# Voice Dimensions Framework

Every brand voice is plotted across 8 measurable dimensions. Each dimension is a spectrum, not a binary. The combination of scores creates a unique voice fingerprint that two writers can independently follow and produce recognizably similar content.

---

## Dimension 1: Formality (Casual ←→ Formal)

**What it measures:** Register, structural conventions, and how much the writing resembles spoken language vs. written language.

**How to detect from a writing sample:**
- Count contractions per 100 words (high = casual)
- Check for sentence fragments (present = casual)
- Look for first-person pronouns and direct address (high = casual)
- Note paragraph length and structural formality (headers, bullet points = more formal structure, not necessarily formal tone)

| Score | Sounds Like | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | "Look, here's the deal." | Texting a colleague you like |
| 5 | "Here's what we found." | A well-written blog post |
| 8 | "Our findings indicate the following." | A white paper or annual report |

**Rules at different levels:**
- **1-3:** Contractions always. Sentence fragments welcome. "And" and "But" start sentences freely. Slang acceptable.
- **4-6:** Contractions usually. Complete sentences preferred but not required. Conversational but structured.
- **7-10:** Contractions rare or absent. Complete sentences always. Third person may replace first person. Passive voice acceptable in moderation.

---

## Dimension 2: Humor (Dry/None ←→ Playful)

**What it measures:** The presence and style of wit, levity, wordplay, and fun in the writing.

**How to detect from a writing sample:**
- Look for unexpected word choices or phrasing
- Note rhetorical questions used for effect (not information)
- Check for self-deprecation, irony, or playful exaggeration
- Absence of humor is a signal too — some brands are intentionally serious

| Score | Sounds Like | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | Dry wit, rare and understated | "This is not, as they say, rocket science." |
| 5 | Occasional levity, natural and earned | "We spent three months on this. Our families missed us." |
| 8 | Playful throughout, personality-forward | "Spreadsheets? In this economy? We thought you'd never ask." |

**Rules at different levels:**
- **1-2:** Humor is nearly absent. When it appears, it is deadpan and subtle. Never forced.
- **3-5:** Humor appears naturally in transitions, asides, and examples. Never in headlines or CTAs unless the brand is built on it.
- **6-8:** Humor is a core feature. Wordplay, pop culture references, and playful tone are expected. The risk of trying too hard is real — humor must still serve clarity.
- **9-10:** The voice is comedy-forward. Almost everything has a twist. This only works for specific brands (entertainment, novelty products, meme-native brands).

---

## Dimension 3: Jargon (Plain Language ←→ Technical)

**What it measures:** Density of industry-specific terminology, acronyms, and insider language.

**How to detect from a writing sample:**
- Count terms that require domain knowledge to understand
- Check whether jargon is defined on first use or assumed known
- Note the ratio of concrete to abstract language

| Score | Sounds Like | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | "This tool helps you send better emails." | Consumer-facing, no assumptions |
| 5 | "Improve your email deliverability with domain authentication." | Practitioner-level, some terms expected |
| 8 | "Configure SPF, DKIM, and DMARC records to optimize inbox placement rates." | Expert-level, jargon is precision |

**Rules at different levels:**
- **1-3:** Explain everything. No acronyms without expansion. Analogies replace technical concepts.
- **4-6:** Use common industry terms freely. Define specialized ones. Assume the reader works in the field but is not an expert.
- **7-10:** Jargon is expected and valued. Simplifying would feel condescending. The reader is a peer.

---

## Dimension 4: Sentence Length (Short/Punchy ←→ Long/Complex)

**What it measures:** Average sentence complexity, clause count, and reading rhythm.

**How to detect from a writing sample:**
- Calculate average words per sentence
- Count sentences with 2+ clauses
- Note the rhythm pattern (do sentences alternate long/short, or stay consistent?)

| Score | Sounds Like | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | "Short sentences. Clear ideas. No fluff." | Average 8-10 words per sentence |
| 5 | "We keep things clear, but we are not afraid of a longer sentence when the idea needs room to breathe." | Average 14-18 words |
| 8 | "Our approach integrates multiple perspectives into a cohesive framework that allows stakeholders to evaluate options against established criteria before committing resources." | Average 22+ words |

**Rules at different levels:**
- **1-3:** Cap most sentences at 12 words. One idea per sentence. Fragments are fine. Rhythm comes from repetition.
- **4-6:** Mix lengths. Short sentences for emphasis, longer ones for explanation. Average 14-18 words.
- **7-10:** Complex ideas get complex sentences. Semicolons and em dashes are tools, not crutches. Paragraphs may be denser.

---

## Dimension 5: Directness (Diplomatic ←→ Blunt)

**What it measures:** How quickly the writing gets to the point and how much hedging or softening is used.

**How to detect from a writing sample:**
- Count hedging words ("might," "perhaps," "could potentially," "it seems")
- Check where the main point appears in a paragraph (first sentence = direct, last = building)
- Note qualifiers and disclaimers

| Score | Sounds Like | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | "There are several factors to consider, and depending on your situation..." | Diplomatic, layered |
| 5 | "Here's what works for most teams, though your mileage may vary." | Direct with context |
| 8 | "Stop doing this. It does not work. Do this instead." | Blunt, point-first |

**Rules at different levels:**
- **1-3:** Lead with context. Acknowledge complexity. Use "we recommend" over "do this."
- **4-6:** Lead with the point, then provide context. One qualifier per paragraph max.
- **7-10:** Point first, always. No hedging. Commands are acceptable. "Maybe" and "perhaps" are banned.

---

## Dimension 6: Warmth (Neutral ←→ Personal)

**What it measures:** Emotional closeness, empathy, and the sense of a human relationship between writer and reader.

**How to detect from a writing sample:**
- Count second-person pronouns ("you," "your")
- Look for empathy markers ("we know this is frustrating," "you are not alone in this")
- Note whether the writing acknowledges the reader's feelings or only their needs
- Check for personal anecdotes or shared experiences

| Score | Sounds Like | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | "The system processes requests in order." | Neutral, informational |
| 5 | "You will see your request processed within a few minutes." | Warm but professional |
| 8 | "We know waiting is frustrating — hang tight, your request is next in line and we are on it." | Personal, empathetic |

**Rules at different levels:**
- **1-3:** Information-first. Emotions are implied, not stated. The writing respects the reader by being efficient.
- **4-6:** Acknowledge the reader as a person. Use "you" and "your" freely. Occasional empathy.
- **7-10:** The reader is a friend. Acknowledge feelings. Share experiences. Use "we" to create solidarity. Personal touches in every piece.

---

## Dimension 7: Authority (Peer ←→ Expert)

**What it measures:** How the writer positions themselves relative to the reader — as an equal sharing what they know, or as an expert guiding from above.

**How to detect from a writing sample:**
- Check the implied hierarchy ("Let me show you" = expert; "Here is what we have been trying" = peer)
- Note whether claims are supported with evidence/credentials or stated as obvious truths
- Look for teaching vs. sharing language

| Score | Sounds Like | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | "We have been experimenting with this and here is what happened." | Peer, sharing openly |
| 5 | "In our experience working with 50+ teams, this pattern consistently works." | Credentialed but accessible |
| 8 | "The correct approach is X. Here is why, and here is how to implement it." | Expert, definitive |

**Rules at different levels:**
- **1-3:** Share, do not teach. Use "we" and "I have found" over "you should." Invite disagreement.
- **4-6:** Teach with credentials. Reference experience naturally. "Based on what we have seen" is your go-to frame.
- **7-10:** State with confidence. Prescribe. "Do this" is acceptable. Credentials are assumed, not restated.

---

## Dimension 8: Energy (Calm ←→ Intense)

**What it measures:** Pace, urgency, enthusiasm, and the emotional temperature of the writing.

**How to detect from a writing sample:**
- Count exclamation points (sparingly = calm, frequently = high energy)
- Note urgency language ("now," "immediately," "do not wait," "this changes everything")
- Check for superlatives ("best," "incredible," "game-changing")
- Assess the pacing — does the writing build slowly or hit hard immediately?

| Score | Sounds Like | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | "Here is a useful update." | Calm, measured, steady |
| 5 | "We just shipped something we are really proud of." | Enthusiastic but grounded |
| 8 | "This changes everything. Seriously. Drop what you are doing and read this." | High energy, urgency-driven |

**Rules at different levels:**
- **1-3:** No exclamation points. No superlatives. Enthusiasm is expressed through specificity, not volume.
- **4-6:** Occasional exclamation points (1 per piece max). Enthusiasm is natural and earned. Superlatives are rare and backed by evidence.
- **7-10:** Energy is a feature. Exclamation points are welcome. Superlatives are strategic. The reader should feel the writer's excitement. Risk: fatigue if everything is at 10.
