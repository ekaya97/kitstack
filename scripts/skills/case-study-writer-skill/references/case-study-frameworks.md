# Case Study Frameworks — Deep Reference

This document details three frameworks for structuring case studies. Each framework has a different narrative emphasis. Choose based on the engagement type and the story you want to tell.

---

## Framework 1: Challenge-Solution-Result (CSR)

### Overview
The most common and versatile case study framework. It follows a simple narrative arc: the client had a problem, you provided a solution, and the results were measurable.

### When to Use
- Default framework for most case studies
- Works for any industry, service type, or engagement length
- Best when results are clear and quantifiable
- Ideal when the story is straightforward: problem → fix → outcome

### Structure in Detail

**Section 1: The Challenge (20% of word count)**

Purpose: Create empathy and recognition. The reader should think "We have that same problem."

- Open with the client's situation — not your involvement
- Describe the specific pain point or trigger that led them to seek help
- Include the business impact of the problem (lost revenue, wasted time, missed opportunities)
- Mention what they'd tried before (if applicable) — this positions your solution as different

Writing tips:
- Use the past tense: "Their team was spending..." not "Their team spends..."
- Include a specific metric that quantifies the pain: "Manual reporting consumed 15 hours per week across the team"
- Don't dramatize — let the facts create urgency
- End the section with the moment they decided to act: "That's when they reached out for help"

**Section 2: The Solution (30% of word count)**

Purpose: Demonstrate your expertise and methodology. The reader should think "These people know what they're doing."

- Describe your approach phase by phase
- Explain WHY you chose this approach — not just WHAT you did
- Include enough detail to prove competence, but not so much that you give away your proprietary process
- Mention tools, frameworks, or methodologies by name (adds credibility)
- Acknowledge any challenges or pivots during the engagement — this adds authenticity

Writing tips:
- Use active voice: "We conducted" not "An audit was conducted"
- Include one surprising insight from your work: "The audit revealed that 60% of their traffic was bouncing from the pricing page — a problem they didn't know they had"
- Break into sub-phases if the engagement was multi-phase
- Don't list every task — highlight the strategic decisions and pivots

**Section 3: The Results (30% of word count)**

Purpose: Prove the value with data. The reader should think "I want that outcome."

- Lead with the most impressive metric
- Present 3-5 results with specific numbers
- Show before/after comparisons
- Include the timeframe for results
- Connect each metric to a business outcome

Writing tips:
- Use a results table for visual impact
- Pair percentages with absolutes: "23% increase (€180,000)"
- Include both primary and secondary outcomes
- Add a qualitative result: "The team reported feeling more confident in their data"

**Section 4: Client Quote (10% of word count)**

Purpose: Add a human voice and third-party validation.

- Place after results for maximum impact
- The quote should address working with you, not just the outcome
- 2-3 sentences maximum

**Section 5: CTA (10% of word count)**

Purpose: Convert the reader.

- One clear action: schedule a call, request a proposal, view related work
- Tie it to the result: "Want to see similar results for your business?"

## Framework 2: STAR (Situation-Task-Action-Result)

### Overview
Borrowed from behavioral interviewing, STAR provides more context about the starting conditions and explicitly separates what needed to be done (Task) from how you did it (Action). This framework suits complex engagements where the situation and your specific role need detailed explanation.

### When to Use
- Complex, multi-phase engagements
- Consulting or advisory projects where context matters heavily
- When your specific role or contribution needs to be distinguished from the broader project
- When the situation involves industry-specific complexity that needs explaining
- Awards submissions that require detailed methodology

### Structure in Detail

**Section 1: Situation (20% of word count)**

The broader context. Not just the problem — the full landscape.

- Industry and market conditions
- Company size, stage, and relevant history
- What was happening internally (team changes, strategic shifts, growth)
- External factors (competition, regulation, market trends)
- How long the situation had been developing

This section is more expansive than CSR's "Challenge" — it paints a complete picture.

**Section 2: Task (15% of word count)**

What specifically needed to be accomplished. This bridges the situation and your actions.

- What was the objective? (Be specific: "Reduce customer acquisition cost from $145 to under $90 within 6 months")
- What constraints existed? (Budget, timeline, technical, organizational)
- What success criteria were defined?
- What was in scope and out of scope?

This section is absent in CSR but critical for complex engagements where "what needed to be done" isn't obvious from the situation alone.

**Section 3: Action (30% of word count)**

What you specifically did. This is more detailed than CSR's "Solution."

- Phase-by-phase breakdown of your work
- Decisions made and why
- Challenges encountered and how you overcame them
- Team composition and roles
- Tools and methodologies used
- Timeline of major milestones

This section is where STAR shines — it lets you demonstrate depth of expertise.

**Section 4: Result (25% of word count)**

Quantified outcomes plus broader impact.

- Primary metrics achieved
- Secondary benefits
- Unexpected positive outcomes
- Long-term impact beyond the immediate engagement
- Client's own assessment of the results

**Section 5: Quote + CTA (10% of word count)**

Same as CSR framework.

## Framework 3: Before/After

### Overview
The most visual and emotionally impactful framework. It focuses entirely on the contrast between the starting state and the ending state. Minimal process detail — maximum transformation evidence.

### When to Use
- The visual transformation is dramatic (design, branding, websites)
- You have strong before/after data (metrics, screenshots, photos)
- The reader doesn't need to understand your process — they need to see the result
- Portfolio pages and visual showcases
- Social media case studies (LinkedIn, Instagram)
- When brevity is important (400-600 words)

### Structure in Detail

**Section 1: The Before (30% of word count)**

What things looked like before your involvement.

- Describe the starting state vividly — use specific details
- Include a "before" metric or data point
- If visual: include or suggest a before screenshot/photo
- Make the reader feel the pain of the before state

Presentation format:
```
### Before
- Website conversion rate: 1.2%
- Average page load: 6.8 seconds
- Mobile bounce rate: 78%
- Monthly organic traffic: 2,300 visits
```

**Section 2: What Changed (15% of word count)**

Brief explanation of the transformation — just enough to connect before and after.

- 2-3 sentences about your approach
- Not a detailed methodology — a bridge between states
- "We redesigned the checkout flow, migrated to a faster hosting platform, and implemented a mobile-first responsive design"

**Section 3: The After (30% of word count)**

What things look like now.

- Mirror the before metrics with after metrics
- Include a visual if applicable (after screenshot/photo)
- Show the contrast explicitly

Presentation format:
```
### After
- Website conversion rate: 3.4% (+183%)
- Average page load: 1.9 seconds (-72%)
- Mobile bounce rate: 34% (-56%)
- Monthly organic traffic: 8,700 visits (+278%)
```

**Section 4: The Impact (15% of word count)**

Translate the metrics into business outcomes.

- "The conversion rate improvement generates an additional €14,000 in monthly revenue"
- "The team now deploys 3x per day, shipping features that used to take weeks"
- Connect the numbers to what the client actually cares about

**Section 5: Quote + CTA (10% of word count)**

Same as other frameworks.

## Choosing the Right Framework

| Question | If Yes → |
|----------|----------|
| Is the story straightforward (problem → solution → result)? | CSR |
| Is the engagement complex with important context? | STAR |
| Is the transformation visual or metrics-driven? | Before/After |
| Is this for a portfolio page with limited space? | Before/After |
| Is this for a detailed marketing page or sales deck? | CSR |
| Is this for an awards submission or thought leadership? | STAR |

When in doubt, default to CSR. It works for everything and readers are familiar with the structure.

---

## Framework Mixing

You can combine elements from different frameworks:

- **CSR with a Before/After results section:** Use the CSR narrative but present results in a before/after comparison format
- **STAR with a condensed structure:** Use STAR's Situation/Task depth but keep Action brief for shorter case studies
- **Before/After with a process sidebar:** Keep the main narrative as Before/After but add a sidebar or callout box explaining the methodology

The frameworks are guidelines, not rigid templates. Adapt to the story.
