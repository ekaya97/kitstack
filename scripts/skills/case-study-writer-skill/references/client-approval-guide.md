# Client Approval Guide for Case Studies

This document covers the process of getting client approval, handling quotes, anonymization, and the legal and relational considerations of publishing case studies.

---

## Why Approval Matters

Publishing a case study without client approval is:
- **A trust violation.** Even if the results are positive, using someone's business as a marketing asset without permission damages the relationship.
- **A potential legal issue.** Depending on your contract and jurisdiction, sharing client details could breach confidentiality clauses.
- **Bad for your reputation.** Word travels. Other clients will wonder: "Will they publish my information without asking?"

Always get written approval. Always.

---

## The Approval Process

### Step 1: Ask Permission (Before Writing)

Timing: After the engagement is complete and results are confirmed.

**Email template:**

```
Subject: Quick question — would you be open to a case study?

Hi [Name],

I'm really proud of the work we did together on [project] and the results 
you've achieved ([headline metric]). I'd love to write it up as a case 
study for our portfolio.

Here's what that would involve:
- I'll draft the case study and send it to you for review
- You'll have full approval rights — nothing publishes without your OK
- I can anonymize any details you'd prefer to keep private
- It would include a link to your company (great for SEO)

Would you be open to this? Happy to discuss what would and wouldn't 
be included.

[Your name]
```

**Key elements:**
- Ask, don't tell
- Emphasize their approval rights
- Offer anonymization upfront
- Mention a benefit to them (backlink, exposure)
- Keep it short — this is a permission request, not a pitch

### Step 2: Write the Draft

Once you have verbal or written agreement:
- Write the complete case study
- Use real details where approved, placeholders where uncertain
- Draft 1-2 quotes for them (see Quote Handling below)
- Flag any sections that contain sensitive information

### Step 3: Send for Review

**Email template:**

```
Subject: Case study draft — your review

Hi [Name],

Here's the draft case study. Please review and let me know:

1. Is anything inaccurate? I want to make sure I got the details right.
2. Is there anything you'd like me to change, add, or remove?
3. Are you comfortable with the quote I've drafted? Feel free to 
   reword it or suggest a different one.
4. Are you OK with using [Company Name], or would you prefer 
   we anonymize?

No rush — whenever you have 10 minutes. And of course, nothing 
publishes until you give the green light.

[Your name]
```

### Step 4: Incorporate Feedback

- Make all requested changes without pushback (this is their story too)
- If they ask to remove a key metric, find a way to work without it
- If they want to rewrite the quote, let them — authenticity matters more than polish
- Send the revised version with tracked changes or highlighted edits

### Step 5: Get Written Approval

A simple email confirmation is sufficient for most cases:

```
Subject: Final case study — approval

Hi [Name],

Here's the final version with your changes incorporated: [link]

If you're happy with it, a quick reply confirming approval is all I need. 
I'll send you the live link once it's published.

Thanks for being part of this!

[Your name]
```

For enterprise clients or sensitive industries, use a formal release form (see below).

## Quote Handling

### The Reality About Client Quotes

Most clients won't write their own quotes. If you ask "Can you write a testimonial?" you'll either get nothing, a one-line generic comment ("Great to work with!"), or something that takes 3 months because it's at the bottom of their priority list.

### The Professional Approach: Draft Quotes for Approval

Write 1-2 quotes that the client can approve, modify, or replace. This is standard practice in B2B marketing.

**How to draft effective quotes:**

1. **Base them on real conversations.** If the client said "This saved us so much time" in a meeting, expand that into a proper quote.
2. **Make them sound like the client, not like a brochure.** Natural language, not marketing speak.
3. **Include a specific detail.** "The dashboard they built saves our team at least 10 hours a week" is better than "They did great work."
4. **Address the experience, not just the result.** "What impressed me was how quickly they understood our business" adds a dimension that metrics alone don't.

**Quote templates:**

**Results-focused:**
> "Since launching the new [deliverable], we've seen [specific metric]. The ROI was clear within the first [timeframe]."

**Process-focused:**
> "What stood out was their approach to [specific methodology]. They took the time to understand our [industry/situation] before jumping into solutions."

**Relationship-focused:**
> "Working with [you/your company] felt like having a senior [role] on our team. They challenged our assumptions in a constructive way and delivered results that exceeded our expectations."

### Quote Attribution

| Level | Format | When to Use |
|-------|--------|-------------|
| Full attribution | "Sarah Mueller, Head of Marketing, TechCorp GmbH" | Client is comfortable with full visibility |
| Partial attribution | "Head of Marketing, B2B SaaS Company" | Client approves the quote but wants company anonymized |
| Title only | "VP of Engineering at a mid-size fintech" | Client wants personal name hidden |
| No attribution | Omit the quote section | Client declines any quote |

## Anonymization

### When to Anonymize

- Client's NDA prohibits naming them
- Client prefers privacy
- The project involved sensitive data or competitive information
- Client is in a regulated industry (healthcare, finance, government)
- Client hasn't responded to approval requests (don't publish named without approval)

### Anonymization Levels

**Level 1: Company name only**
- Replace company name with descriptor: "A leading B2B SaaS company"
- Keep all other details (industry, size, location, metrics)
- Sufficient for most cases

**Level 2: Company + details**
- Replace company name with descriptor
- Adjust company size to a range: "200-500 employees" instead of "340 employees"
- Round metrics to remove identifiable precision
- Change location to region: "DACH region" instead of "Munich"

**Level 3: Full anonymization**
- Generic industry descriptor: "A mid-size technology company"
- All metrics presented as ranges or percentages only
- No location details
- No specific product or service names that could identify the client

### What to Keep Even When Anonymizing
- The industry (at minimum, the general sector)
- The general company size (startup, mid-market, enterprise)
- The results (anonymize the context, not the outcome)
- Your methodology and approach

Without these, the case study loses all credibility. A case study about "a company that had some problem and we helped them" has zero marketing value.

## Formal Release Form

For enterprise clients, regulated industries, or when you want extra protection, use a formal release:

```
CASE STUDY RELEASE AGREEMENT

I, [Client Name], [Title] at [Company], authorize [Your Company] to use 
the following in marketing materials including but not limited to website, 
social media, presentations, and print materials:

☐ Company name
☐ Company logo
☐ Project description and methodology
☐ Quantified results and metrics
☐ Direct quotes attributed to me
☐ Other: _______________

I have reviewed the attached case study draft dated [date] and approve 
its publication.

I understand that:
- I may request modifications at any time
- I may revoke this permission with 30 days written notice
- [Your Company] will remove the case study within 30 days of revocation

Signature: _______________ Date: _______________
Name: _______________
Title: _______________
Company: _______________
```

This is optional for most freelancer/agency relationships. A simple email "looks good, go ahead" is sufficient in most cases. Reserve the formal release for clients who request it or when dealing with large organizations.

## Offering Value in Return

Clients are more likely to approve case studies when there's something in it for them:

| Incentive | When to Offer |
|-----------|---------------|
| Backlink to their website | Always — easy, valuable for SEO |
| Social media co-promotion | When publishing on LinkedIn or social |
| Featured placement on your portfolio | When you have high-traffic pages |
| Early access to the published piece for their own marketing | Always |
| A discount on future work | Rarely — can feel transactional |
| Co-authored blog post or thought leadership | For high-profile clients |

The most effective incentive is simply the backlink and the implied endorsement. Most clients appreciate being featured as a success story — it reflects well on their decision-making.

## Timing the Ask

| Situation | When to Ask |
|-----------|-------------|
| Project just completed successfully | Within 1-2 weeks of final delivery (enthusiasm is highest) |
| Ongoing retainer with strong results | At a quarterly review or when hitting a milestone |
| Client mentioned being happy | Immediately — "Would you be open to a case study?" |
| Client gave you a referral | Shortly after — they're already advocating for you |
| 6+ months after engagement | Still OK, but remind them of the specific results first |

The best time to ask is when the client has just seen the results and is feeling positive about the engagement. Don't wait 6 months — the details fade and the enthusiasm drops.
