# Client Objection Handlers

Patterns for addressing common client pushback during the proposal process. These frameworks should be woven into the proposal structure proactively — the best proposals answer objections before they're raised.

---

## 1. Price Objections

### "This is more than we expected / more than our budget"

**Root cause:** Sticker shock, or genuine budget constraint.

**In the proposal (proactive):**
- Show the breakdown — a total feels arbitrary, but line items feel justified
- Include a value calculation: "Your investment of $18,000 targets a project that will serve your business for 3-5 years, equating to $300-500/month"
- Offer a phased approach: "Phase 1 ($X) can be executed independently. Phases 2-3 can be scheduled based on Phase 1 results."

**In conversation (reactive):**
- "I understand. Let me walk through what drives the investment. [Walk through phases.] If the total needs to come down, we can adjust scope — which of these phases is most critical for you right now?"
- Never drop price without removing scope. Dropping price without changing scope signals your original price was inflated.

### "Competitor X quoted us $Y (lower)"

**Root cause:** Price comparison without scope comparison.

**Response framework:**
- "That's useful context. A few questions to make sure we're comparing the same scope: Does their quote include [specific deliverable]? How many revision rounds? What's their timeline? Who on their team would lead the project?"
- Position your price through specificity: "Our quote includes X, Y, and Z, which are often not included in lower quotes and lead to additional costs mid-project."

### "Can you do it for a flat $X?"

**Root cause:** Client wants budget certainty.

**Response framework:**
- If $X is reasonable: "Yes, and here's what the scope looks like at that investment level. [Adjust scope to fit.]"
- If $X is too low: "At $X, we could deliver [reduced scope]. For the full scope you described, the investment is $Y. Would you like me to propose a phased approach that starts within your budget?"

## 2. Timeline Objections

### "We need this faster"

**Root cause:** Internal deadline pressure, or testing your flexibility.

**In the proposal (proactive):**
- Show critical path dependencies: "Weeks 3-4 require client feedback on designs. The timeline assumes 3 business days for each feedback round."
- Offer a fast-track option at premium: "Fast-track delivery (compressed by 2 weeks) is available at a 20% premium to cover parallel workstreams and priority scheduling."

**In conversation (reactive):**
- "We can compress the timeline. There are two ways: (1) We run phases in parallel instead of sequentially — this requires more availability from your team for concurrent feedback. (2) We add team members to reduce per-phase duration — this adds approximately 15-20% to the investment. Which approach works better for you?"
- Never promise a faster timeline without explaining the trade-off (cost, quality, or client involvement).

### "Why does this take so long?"

**Root cause:** Client doesn't understand the process.

**Response framework:**
- Walk through the timeline and explain what happens in each phase: "Week 1-2 is discovery — we interview your stakeholders and audit your current materials. This is where we build the foundation. Skipping this phase is the #1 reason projects go over budget later."
- Compare to their experience: "You mentioned your last agency delivered in 4 weeks but the result didn't meet expectations. Our 7-week timeline includes built-in review cycles so we course-correct along the way."

## 3. Scope Objections

### "Can you include [additional thing] in the same budget?"

**Root cause:** Scope creep, or genuine misunderstanding of what's included.

**In the proposal (proactive):**
- Include a clear "Out of Scope" section
- Add a change order clause: "Changes to scope are accommodated through a simple change order process. We'll provide a written estimate for any additions before work begins."

**In conversation (reactive):**
- "Absolutely, we can include [thing]. That would add approximately $X to the investment and Y days to the timeline. Want me to revise the proposal with that included?"
- Never say "no" — always say "yes, and here's what that looks like."

### "We're not sure exactly what we need yet"

**Root cause:** Client hasn't defined their requirements.

**Response framework:**
- Propose a paid discovery phase: "That's completely normal at this stage. I'd recommend starting with a 2-week Discovery phase ($X). At the end, you'll have a clear requirements document and a refined proposal for the execution phase. No commitment beyond Discovery."
- This converts an uncertain prospect into a paying client and gives you scope clarity for the larger proposal.

### "We want to start with a small test project"

**Root cause:** Risk mitigation — they want to see your work before committing to the full scope.

**Response framework:**
- Offer Phase 1 as a standalone: "Great approach. Phase 1 (Discovery & Audit) works well as a standalone engagement at $X. You'll receive [deliverables] and can decide whether to continue to Phase 2 based on the results."
- Price the test fairly — don't discount it heavily. If they're testing quality, delivering at a discount signals lower quality.

## 4. Trust / Credibility Objections

### "We've been burned by agencies/freelancers before"

**Root cause:** Past negative experience. Need reassurance.

**In the proposal (proactive):**
- Milestone-based payments tied to deliverables (they only pay when they see results)
- Specific revision rounds defined (they know they'll get chances to course-correct)
- Communication cadence stated (weekly check-ins, bi-weekly demos)
- Named team member / point of contact (not "a team member will be assigned")

**In conversation (reactive):**
- "I hear that a lot, and it's exactly why we structure our engagements with milestone payments. You approve each phase deliverable before we invoice for the next phase. If at any point the work doesn't meet the agreed standards, we revise before moving forward."

### "Can we see examples of similar work?"

**Root cause:** Legitimate due diligence.

**Response framework:**
- Include 2-3 relevant case studies or portfolio links in the proposal's About section
- If you can't share client work (NDA): "We've completed similar engagements for companies in [industry]. While we can't share the work publicly due to NDAs, I can walk you through our approach and results on a call."

### "Why should we hire you instead of a bigger agency?"

**Root cause:** Perceived risk of working with a smaller provider.

**Response framework:**
- "With a larger agency, your project would be managed by a junior account manager and executed by whoever is available. With us, [Name] leads the project directly — the same person who wrote this proposal will be doing the work. That means faster decisions, fewer layers, and senior expertise on every deliverable."

## 5. Decision / Timing Objections

### "We need to think about it / discuss internally"

**Root cause:** Not enough urgency, or genuine internal process.

**In the proposal (proactive):**
- Include proposal validity: "This proposal is valid for 30 days from [date]."
- Mention your availability: "Our next available start date is [date]. We reserve this slot for 10 business days from proposal delivery."

**In conversation (reactive):**
- "Of course — take the time you need. A few things that might help your discussion: [summarize 2-3 key value points]. I'm available for a 15-minute call with anyone else on your team who has questions."
- Always offer to present to the decision-making group.

### "We're also talking to other providers"

**Root cause:** Normal buying behavior, price shopping.

**Response framework:**
- "That makes sense — you should compare approaches. I'd suggest looking at three things beyond price: (1) Who specifically will do the work? (2) What's their process for handling scope changes? (3) Can they show results from a similar project? Happy to answer any of those for our approach."
- Don't badmouth competitors. Don't panic-discount.

### "The timing isn't right — maybe next quarter"

**Root cause:** Budget cycle, priorities shifted, or polite decline.

**Response framework:**
- "Understood. Would it be helpful if I sent you a revised timeline for a Q[X] start? That way you'll have a ready-to-go proposal when the timing is right."
- If they agree, stay in touch with a brief monthly check-in. If they don't respond, it was likely a polite no.

## 6. Contract / Terms Objections

### "We need to use our standard contract instead of yours"

**Response:** "Happy to review your standard terms. I'll flag anything that significantly affects the project structure — things like payment terms, IP ownership, and liability caps. In most cases we can work within your framework with minor adjustments."

### "30-day payment terms don't work for us — we do net 60"

**Response:** "We can accommodate net 60 terms. To offset the extended payment cycle, we'd adjust the payment structure to include a larger upfront milestone — typically 40% on signing rather than 30%."

### "Who owns the IP?"

**Response:** "Upon full payment, all deliverables and intellectual property transfer to you completely. During the project, we retain working rights to complete the deliverables. We may request permission to use the work in our portfolio — but that's your call, and we'll never share confidential materials."
