# Competitive Analysis Frameworks

Four frameworks for analyzing competitive dynamics. Use the one that fits the user's situation, or combine them for a comprehensive teardown.

---

## 1. Porter's Five Forces (Adapted for Small Business)

The original framework was designed for Fortune 500 strategy. Here's the version that matters for startups, freelancers, and small agencies.

### Force 1: Competitive Rivalry (Direct Competitors)

**Questions to answer:**
- How many competitors are in this space?
- Are they growing, stable, or declining?
- How similar are the offerings? (If very similar, competition is on price. If differentiated, competition is on positioning.)
- What's the switching cost for customers? (High = less rivalry. Low = constant churn risk.)
- How aggressive is competitor marketing? Are they outspending you?

**Assessment scale:**
| Level | Meaning | Implication |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Low rivalry | Few competitors, differentiated offerings | Pricing power, room to grow |
| Medium rivalry | Several competitors, some overlap | Need clear positioning |
| High rivalry | Many competitors, similar offerings, price pressure | Must differentiate or niche down aggressively |

**Small business adjustment:** For solopreneurs and small agencies, rivalry is measured locally, not globally. A freelance designer in Munich competes with 50 designers in Munich, not 50,000 globally. Define the geographic and segment boundaries before assessing rivalry.

### Force 2: Threat of New Entrants (Barrier to Entry)

**Questions to answer:**
- How hard is it for someone new to enter this market?
- What does it take to compete? (Capital, technology, brand, relationships, certifications?)
- Are new entrants appearing? At what rate?
- Does AI reduce the barrier to entry for your offering?

**Assessment:**
| Barrier level | Examples | Implication |
|---------------|---------|-------------|
| Low | Freelance services, content creation, basic SaaS | Must compete on brand, speed, or niche |
| Medium | Specialized consulting, regulated industries, B2B with long sales cycles | Existing players have advantage, but determined entrants can break in |
| High | Deep tech, hardware, government contracts, enterprise with procurement requirements | Incumbents are protected, but also slow to innovate |

**Small business adjustment:** For most small businesses, barriers are low. This means your moat is not your product — it's your relationships, your reputation, and your speed. Plan accordingly.

### Force 3: Threat of Substitutes (Indirect Competition)

**Questions to answer:**
- What do customers do if your product/service didn't exist? (Spreadsheets, manual process, different type of product entirely)
- Is there a "good enough" alternative that's free or cheaper?
- Could AI tools replace part of what you do?
- Could the customer bring this in-house?

**Assessment:**
| Substitute threat | Example | Implication |
|------------------|---------|-------------|
| Low | Customer has no real alternative | Strong pricing power, focus on retention |
| Medium | Alternatives exist but are inferior or require more effort | Demonstrate the cost of the alternative (time, quality, risk) |
| High | Free or cheap alternatives are "good enough" for most customers | Must target customers who need more than "good enough" — premium segment |

**This is the most underestimated force.** Most businesses focus on direct competitors while losing customers to "we'll just use a spreadsheet" or "we'll hire an intern."

### Force 4: Buyer Power (Customer Leverage)

**Questions to answer:**
- Can your customers easily switch to a competitor?
- Are there a few large customers (they have power) or many small customers (you have power)?
- Do customers know what alternatives exist and what they cost?
- Can customers combine purchasing power (e.g., RFP processes)?

**Assessment:**
| Buyer power | Meaning | Implication |
|-------------|---------|-------------|
| Low | Many small customers, switching costs, limited alternatives | Pricing flexibility, subscription models work |
| Medium | Customers have alternatives, moderate switching costs | Must justify value, retention is key |
| High | Few large customers, easy switching, transparent pricing | Negotiate hard, diversify customer base, invest in switching costs (integrations, data lock-in) |

### Force 5: Supplier Power (Your Dependencies)

**Questions to answer:**
- What platforms, tools, or providers does your business depend on?
- Could they raise prices, change terms, or compete with you?
- Are you dependent on a single platform (e.g., Shopify, AWS, LinkedIn)?

**Assessment:**
| Supplier power | Example | Implication |
|---------------|---------|-------------|
| Low | Multiple alternatives for every tool/platform | Negotiate aggressively, switch freely |
| Medium | Some dependencies, but alternatives exist with switching cost | Plan migration paths, don't over-invest in one platform |
| High | Platform dependency (all revenue from one channel) | Diversify urgently, build owned channels |

## 2. SWOT Analysis (Done Right)

SWOT is the most common and most commonly useless framework. Here's how to make it actually useful.

### The Problem With Most SWOTs
- Entries are vague: "Strong team" is a strength? Compared to what?
- No prioritization: 15 items per quadrant with no ranking
- No action: A list of strengths that leads to no decision is decoration
- Confirmation bias: Strengths are inflated, weaknesses are minimized

### How to Do SWOT Properly

**Rule 1: Every entry must be specific and comparative.**
- Bad: "Innovative product"
- Good: "Only product in the market with real-time collaboration — competitors require export/import workflow"

**Rule 2: Every entry must be evidence-based.**
- Bad: "Strong brand recognition"
- Good: "Ranked #1 on G2 in our category with 4.8/5 average rating (vs. competitor average of 4.2)"

**Rule 3: Limit to 5 items per quadrant, ranked by importance.**

**Rule 4: Cross-reference quadrants to generate strategy.**
- Strength + Opportunity = Aggressive strategy (invest here)
- Strength + Threat = Defensive strategy (protect this)
- Weakness + Opportunity = Development strategy (build capability)
- Weakness + Threat = Survival strategy (avoid or exit)

### SWOT Output Template

| | Helps | Hurts |
|---|-------|-------|
| **Internal** | **Strengths** | **Weaknesses** |
| | 1. [Specific, comparative, evidenced] | 1. [Specific, comparative, evidenced] |
| | 2. ... | 2. ... |
| **External** | **Opportunities** | **Threats** |
| | 1. [Specific, time-bounded] | 1. [Specific, with likelihood] |
| | 2. ... | 2. ... |

## 3. Feature Matrix with Importance Weighting

A feature comparison that indicates which features actually matter to customers.

### How to Build It

**Step 1: Define the dimensions.** These should be the criteria customers use when choosing between options. Not internal capabilities — external-facing value.

Categories to consider:
- Core functionality (what the product/service does)
- Ease of use / experience
- Price / value
- Support / service level
- Integration / compatibility
- Trust / credibility (brand, reviews, track record)

**Step 2: Rate each competitor on each dimension.**

Use a 4-point scale:
- (blank) = Not available / not applicable
- Basic = Has it, but minimal
- Good = Solid implementation, meets expectations
- Best = Category-leading, genuine advantage

**Step 3: Weight the dimensions by customer importance.**

| Weight | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| Critical | Deal-breaker — customers won't buy without this |
| Important | Significantly influences the decision |
| Nice-to-have | Customers notice but won't choose based on this alone |
| Irrelevant | Only matters to a small segment |

**Step 4: Identify the pattern.**
- Where do you lead on Critical dimensions? (This is your pitch.)
- Where do you lag on Critical dimensions? (This is your risk.)
- Where do competitors lead on Nice-to-have dimensions? (Ignore — don't chase features that don't win deals.)

### Feature Matrix Output Template

| Dimension | Weight | You | Competitor A | Competitor B | Status Quo |
|-----------|--------|-----|-------------|-------------|------------|
| [Feature 1] | Critical | Best | Good | Basic | (blank) |
| [Feature 2] | Important | Good | Best | Good | Basic |
| [Feature 3] | Nice-to-have | Basic | Good | Best | (blank) |
| ... | | | | | |

## 4. Value Curve Analysis

A visual framework for finding differentiation opportunities by mapping where competitors over-invest and under-invest.

### How It Works

1. List the 8-12 factors the industry competes on (features, price, service level, speed, customization, etc.)
2. Rate each competitor on each factor (1-5 scale)
3. Plot the curves — each competitor is a line
4. Look for where all competitors cluster (industry consensus) and where gaps exist

### What to Look For

**Convergence zones:** If all competitors rate 4-5 on a factor, the industry has decided this is table stakes. Competing here won't differentiate you — you need to match it.

**Divergence zones:** If competitors vary widely on a factor, the market hasn't decided how much to invest here. This is an opportunity to take a strong position.

**Under-served factors:** If all competitors rate 1-2 on a factor that customers care about, there's a white space opportunity.

**Over-served factors:** If all competitors rate 4-5 on a factor that customers don't actually value much, there's an opportunity to strip it out and redirect investment elsewhere (the Blue Ocean "eliminate" move).

### Value Curve Output Template

| Factor | Industry Average | You | Opportunity |
|--------|-----------------|-----|-------------|
| Price | 3 | 2 (cheaper) | Not differentiated — everyone's price-competitive |
| Support | 2 | 4 | Differentiated — most competitors under-invest in support |
| Customization | 4 | 3 | Behind — need to close this gap or explicitly position as opinionated/simple |
| Speed | 2 | 4 | Differentiated — if customers value speed, this is your lead message |
| ... | | | |

### Blue Ocean Moves

Based on the value curve, identify:
- **Eliminate:** What factors can you stop competing on? (Frees up resources)
- **Reduce:** Where can you invest less than the industry without losing customers?
- **Raise:** Where should you invest more than the industry?
- **Create:** What factor doesn't exist in the industry yet that you can introduce?

## Framework Selection Guide

| Situation | Recommended Framework |
|-----------|----------------------|
| Strategic planning, market entry | Porter's Five Forces |
| Specific competitor comparison | Feature Matrix |
| Finding differentiation angles | Value Curve Analysis |
| Board/investor presentation | SWOT (done properly) + Positioning Map |
| Quick competitive check | Feature Matrix (3-4 dimensions only) |
| Positioning or repositioning | Value Curve + Differentiation Mapping |

## Combining Frameworks

For a comprehensive teardown:
1. **Porter's Five Forces** to understand the market structure
2. **Feature Matrix** to compare specific capabilities
3. **Value Curve** to find differentiation opportunities
4. **SWOT** to synthesize internal vs. external factors

For a quick teardown:
1. **Feature Matrix** (5-6 dimensions, weighted)
2. **Positioning map** (2 axes)
3. **3 recommendations** based on the gaps you see
