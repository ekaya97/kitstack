# Example: Sales Demo Follow-Up

## User Input

> I just did a demo of our project management tool (Buildtrack) for a construction company called Redstone Builders. Demo was with Jake Morrison (Operations Director) and two of his project managers (Tanya and Luis). They currently use a mix of Procore and spreadsheets. Main pain points: field crews don't update Procore because it's too complex on mobile, so PMs spend Monday mornings chasing updates from Friday. They lose about a day per PM per week on status chasing. Also mentioned that Procore's change order process is painful — takes 3-4 days to get a CO approved. They have 12 active projects and 45 field crew leads.
>
> Demo went well. Jake really liked the mobile-first approach — when I showed the one-tap daily update from field crews his exact words were "that alone would save us." Tanya asked about offline mode for rural job sites. I said we support offline with sync when back online and she seemed relieved. Luis asked about Procore data migration — can we import their existing project data? I said yes but need to check on the specifics with our eng team. Jake asked about pricing — I gave the range ($15-25/user/mo depending on plan). He didn't flinch.
>
> They want to trial it on 2 projects first. Jake said he needs to talk to their CFO (Amanda Chen) before committing. I suggested a 30-day pilot. Jake said "send me what you need to get started." I think they'll move if we keep momentum.
>
> Oh, one concern: Luis mentioned their current Procore contract runs through November. Not sure if that blocks them or if they'd run parallel.
>
> My name is Rachel, I'm an account exec.

## Skill's Internal Analysis (Not Shown to User)

**Meeting type:** Sales demo follow-up → advance the deal, reinforce value, clear the path to next step.

**Positive signals to anchor:**
- Jake's exact quote: "that alone would save us" (re: one-tap updates)
- Jake gave "send me what you need to get started" — buying signal
- No price flinch at $15-25/user/month
- 55+ minute engagement with all three attendees

**Questions/objections to address:**
1. Offline mode (Tanya) — answered positively, confirm in writing
2. Procore data migration (Luis) — partial answer, needs follow-up
3. Procore contract through November — potential blocker, needs framing
4. CFO approval needed (Amanda Chen) — need to help Jake sell internally

**Strategic considerations:**
- Jake said "send me what you need" → the email should include a concrete pilot plan, not just a recap
- CFO approval is the real gate → offer to provide ROI materials
- Procore overlap → frame as "parallel run" not "switch immediately"
- Luis's migration question was left partially answered → follow up with specifics to maintain credibility

**Tone:** Confident, helpful, consultative. Rachel is a trusted advisor who showed them something valuable, not a salesperson chasing a signature. The demo went well — the email should maintain that momentum without being pushy.

**Decision: address Procore contract proactively.** Luis raised it as a concern. If the email ignores it, it becomes a silent objection that blocks the deal. Better to frame it as a non-issue ("parallel run") and remove it as a barrier.

## Why This Works

### Opening Strategy

The email opens with Jake's exact words: "that alone would save us." This serves three purposes:

1. **Anchoring** — It reminds Jake of his own enthusiasm. Between the demo and reading this email, competing priorities may have diluted his interest. His own words bring him back to the moment of conviction.
2. **Personalization proof** — This obviously isn't a template. The quote makes it feel like a conversation continuation, not a sales follow-up.
3. **Social validation** — "exactly the feedback we hear from ops directors" normalizes his reaction and subtly tells him that other people like him chose Buildtrack. This is social proof without a case study link.

Note: the email is addressed to Jake but CC'd to Tanya and Luis. Jake is the decision driver, so the email speaks to him primarily. Tanya and Luis are acknowledged by name (Tanya's offline question, Luis's migration question) so they feel included without being the primary audience.

### Benefits, Not Features

The "What stood out" section doesn't list product features. It lists benefits mapped to Redstone's specific pain points:
- "60 seconds from their phone" → solves "crews don't update Procore"
- "close in hours, not days" → solves "3-4 day CO approval"
- "core feature, not a workaround" → addresses Tanya's concern that offline might be an afterthought

Each bullet includes Redstone's numbers (45 crew leads, 3-4 day CO cycle) to make the benefits concrete, not abstract.

### Handling the Unanswered Question

Luis asked about Procore data migration. Rachel said "yes" during the demo but didn't have specifics. The follow-up handles this honestly: "I'm confirming the exact scope with our engineering team" with a specific date for the answer (Thursday). This maintains credibility.

A weaker approach would be to ignore the question and hope Luis forgets. He won't — and unanswered questions erode trust. A worse approach would be to make up specifics. The best approach is what Rachel does: confirm the capability, acknowledge the detail gap, and commit to a date.

### Procore Contract Framing

Luis raised the November contract as a concern. The email reframes it as a non-issue through two moves:
1. **Normalize parallel runs:** "Most teams in your situation keep both platforms active" — this is expected, not unusual
2. **Turn it into an advantage:** "months of side-by-side comparison data" — the overlap becomes a feature, not a cost

The email never says "you should cancel Procore" or "your current tool is outdated." Redstone is currently paying for Procore. Insulting their current choice insults their past judgment.

### The Pilot Plan

Jake said "send me what you need to get started." So the email does exactly that — a concrete, structured pilot plan. This is the critical difference between a follow-up that maintains momentum and one that loses it.

The pilot plan includes:
- **Scope** that's small enough to say yes to (2 projects, not 12)
- **Evaluation criteria** that Redstone would care about (crew adoption, PM time savings, CO cycle time)
- **Support structure** that de-risks the pilot (dedicated onboarding, Slack channel, weekly check-ins)
- **Pricing** that's transparent ($15/user/month — the low end of the range Jake heard)

The evaluation criteria are particularly important: they give Jake specific things to measure, which means the pilot has a clear success/failure metric. This prevents the pilot from drifting without a decision.

### CFO Strategy

The CFO approval is the real gate to the deal. Rachel handles it with two offers:
1. **ROI summary** — arms Jake with numbers for his internal pitch. This is the high-leverage move. Jake may want Buildtrack, but Amanda cares about ROI. Giving Jake a one-pager to hand to Amanda shortens the internal sales cycle.
2. **Join a call** — offers to be available if Amanda has questions. This removes the "telephone game" risk where Jake explains the product imperfectly to Amanda.

Both are framed as "whatever makes it easiest for you" — the message is "I'm here to help you succeed internally," not "let me talk to your boss."

### Single Clear CTA

The email has exactly one path forward: approve the pilot, send the project list, and get started. There's no "Book a follow-up call OR start a trial OR download our case study." Multiple CTAs dilute each other. One clear path converts.

### What's Intentionally Absent

- **No "thanks for your time" opening** — replaced with something that advances the conversation
- **No feature dump** — they saw the demo; a product sheet adds nothing
- **No multiple CTAs** — one path forward (pilot), not three competing options
- **No competitive language about Procore** — the email never says "better than Procore" or "unlike your current tool." Redstone knows the problems. Rachel doesn't need to rub it in.
- **No pressure tactics** — no "limited-time pricing" or "we only have 3 onboarding slots this month." The deal will close on the strength of the product fit, not manufactured scarcity.
- **No internal observations** — "I think they'll move if we keep momentum" is Rachel's private sales note. It stays out of the email.
- **No attachments** — nothing to download or open. Everything is in the email body. Attachments add friction and often go unread.
