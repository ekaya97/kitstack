# Follow-Up Frameworks by Meeting Type

Detailed structure and strategy for each meeting type. Use the framework that matches the user's meeting, then customize based on their specific context.

---

## 1. Client Discovery Call Follow-Up

**Purpose:** Confirm that you understood their needs, build trust, and set up the next step (usually a proposal or deeper conversation).

**Structure:**
1. Opening — reference a specific pain point or goal they shared
2. Recap of their situation — 3-4 sentences showing you understood the problem, not just heard it
3. Your initial thinking — light preview of how you'd approach the work (not a full proposal)
4. Confirmed next steps — what you'll send and when
5. Questions for them — 1-2 things you need before moving forward
6. Warm close — express genuine interest in the project

**Tone:** Attentive, professional, subtly confident. You're demonstrating listening skills and competence without being presumptuous.

**Include:**
- Specific quotes or data points they mentioned ("You mentioned your conversion rate dropped from 3.2% to 1.8% after the redesign")
- Their stated priorities, in their language (don't rephrase into your jargon)
- A concrete deliverable with a date ("I'll send a scope and estimate by Wednesday")

**Exclude:**
- Pricing (too early — save for the proposal)
- Your full methodology (hint at it, don't lecture)
- Assumptions about their budget unless they stated it
- Competitive comparisons ("Unlike other agencies, we...")

**Example subject line:** "Follow-up: [Project Name] — initial thoughts + next steps"

## 2. Internal Strategy / Planning Meeting Follow-Up

**Purpose:** Align the team, document decisions, assign ownership, and prevent the "wait, I thought we agreed on something different" problem.

**Structure:**
1. One-line context — what the meeting was about (for anyone CC'd who wasn't there)
2. Decisions made — numbered list of what was agreed, using definitive language
3. Action items — table with owner, task, and deadline
4. Parking lot — topics raised but deferred
5. Next meeting — date, time, and what needs to happen before then

**Tone:** Efficient, clear, no fluff. Internal emails should respect people's time. Get to the decisions and actions immediately.

**Include:**
- Definitive language for decisions: "We decided to..." / "We agreed that..." / "The direction is..."
- Every action item with a named owner (not a team — a person)
- Specific deadlines, not "next week" (say "by Friday, May 9")
- Parking lot items so nothing is lost

**Exclude:**
- Discussion summaries (people were there — they don't need a replay)
- Hedging language ("I think we may have decided...") — if it wasn't clear, flag it as needing confirmation
- Long preambles or thank-yous (save warmth for external emails)
- CC'ing people who don't have action items (unless they need to be informed)

**Example subject line:** "Q3 planning — decisions + action items [May 5 meeting]"

## 3. Sales Demo / Pitch Follow-Up

**Purpose:** Advance the deal. Reinforce the value they saw, address concerns raised during the demo, and create a clear path to the next step (usually a proposal, trial, or decision-maker meeting).

**Structure:**
1. Opening — reference something specific they reacted positively to during the demo
2. Personalized recap — what you showed and why it matters to their specific situation (not generic features)
3. Answers to their questions — address every question or concern raised, directly and concisely
4. Social proof — one relevant case study or data point (not a list of logos)
5. Clear CTA — one specific next step with a suggested date/time

**Tone:** Confident, helpful, not pushy. You're a trusted advisor who showed them something valuable, not a salesperson chasing a signature.

**Include:**
- A specific moment from the demo that resonated ("When you saw the automated reporting, you mentioned it would save your ops team 6 hours/week")
- Answers to every question they asked (if you don't have an answer yet: "I'm checking with our engineering team and will have an answer by Thursday")
- One relevant case study or metric — make it specific to their industry or use case
- A single, clear call to action ("Would Thursday at 2pm work for a call with your VP of Ops?")

**Exclude:**
- Feature lists (they just saw the demo — they don't need a brochure)
- Multiple CTAs (one next step, not three options)
- Aggressive urgency ("This pricing is only available until Friday!")
- Attachments (link to resources, don't attach PDFs that won't be opened)

**Example subject line:** "Your [Product] demo — recap + answers to your questions"

## 4. Vendor / Partner Meeting Follow-Up

**Purpose:** Confirm what was discussed, document agreed terms or partnership structure, and clarify outstanding items before anything gets formalized.

**Structure:**
1. Opening — reference the shared goal or partnership opportunity
2. What was discussed — brief context of the conversation
3. Agreed terms / points — clear enumeration of what both parties committed to
4. Outstanding items — what still needs to be resolved, with owners
5. Next steps — who does what, by when, to move things forward

**Tone:** Professional, collaborative, precise. Both sides need clarity. This email often becomes the informal record that a formal agreement is later based on.

**Include:**
- Explicit attribution: "You mentioned..." / "We committed to..." / "Both parties agreed..."
- Commercial terms discussed (pricing, revenue splits, timelines) — even if preliminary
- Any contingencies or conditions ("Subject to legal review" / "Pending board approval")
- Specific follow-up actions with owners on both sides

**Exclude:**
- Internal negotiation strategy ("We think we can get them down to...")
- Assumptions beyond what was discussed
- Pressure language ("We need to move quickly on this")
- Legal language (leave that for the contract)

**Example subject line:** "Partnership discussion follow-up — agreed points + next steps"

## 5. Check-In / Status Update Follow-Up

**Purpose:** Maintain momentum, surface blockers, celebrate progress, and confirm the path forward until the next check-in.

**Structure:**
1. Opening — one sentence connecting to the previous check-in or project phase
2. Progress since last meeting — what's been completed or advanced
3. Current status — where things stand right now (on track / at risk / blocked)
4. Blockers or needs — anything that requires the other party's input or action
5. What's next — focus for the next period + next check-in date

**Tone:** Momentum-oriented, transparent, constructive. Even when things are off track, the tone should be solution-focused, not blame-focused.

**Include:**
- Concrete progress markers ("Completed user testing with 8 participants — report attached")
- Status indicators if managing multiple workstreams (On Track / Needs Attention / Blocked)
- Specific asks: "We need access to the staging API by Wednesday to stay on schedule"
- Next check-in date and time

**Exclude:**
- Exhaustive activity logs (share the outcomes, not the hours)
- Excuses for delays (state the delay, state the revised timeline, state what you need)
- Scope change requests buried in a status email (those deserve their own conversation)
- Overly optimistic spin when things are behind (transparency builds more trust than spin)

**Example subject line:** "Project Atlas — Week 3 update: on track, one blocker"

## 6. Kickoff Meeting Follow-Up

**Purpose:** Set the tone for the engagement, confirm roles and responsibilities, establish communication norms, and create excitement about the first milestone.

**Structure:**
1. Opening — express energy about the project starting (genuine, not performative)
2. Project overview — one paragraph confirming the scope and goal
3. Team and roles — who does what, on both sides
4. Communication plan — how and when you'll communicate (tools, cadence, escalation)
5. Key dates — first milestone, first deliverable, first check-in
6. Immediate next steps — what happens in the first 48-72 hours
7. Resources needed — access, logins, documents, contacts required to start

**Tone:** Energetic, organized, forward-looking. This email sets the standard for the entire engagement. If it's sloppy, the client worries about the project. If it's sharp, they feel confident.

**Include:**
- Named contacts on both sides with roles ("Sarah is your day-to-day contact; Jamie handles technical questions")
- Communication expectations ("We'll share weekly updates every Friday. For urgent items, reach us on Slack.")
- The first deliverable and its date ("You'll receive the discovery findings report by May 16")
- Any homework for the client ("Please share access to your analytics dashboard and CRM by Wednesday so we can start the audit")

**Exclude:**
- Restating the entire proposal (they already read it)
- Caveats and disclaimers (this is a confidence-building moment, not a risk-mitigation moment)
- Requests that should have been handled before kickoff
- Overly long bios of team members (a name and role is sufficient)

**Example subject line:** "We're live — [Project Name] kickoff recap + first steps"
