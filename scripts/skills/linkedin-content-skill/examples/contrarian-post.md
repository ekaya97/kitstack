# Example: Contrarian Post

## User Input

> I'm a startup founder (B2B SaaS). I'm tired of seeing advice about "building in public." I've tried it, and it actively hurt my business — competitors copied features, potential clients questioned our stability, and the constant content creation distracted the team. I want to write a nuanced take on why building in public doesn't work for everyone.

---

## Post Analysis

**Post type:** Contrarian
**Hook strategy:** Bold statement (Pattern #7) — direct challenge to a popular belief
**Target audience:** SaaS founders, startup operators
**Goal:** Authority (demonstrating independent thinking backed by experience)

---

## Generated Post

Building in public almost killed our startup.

I know this is LinkedIn heresy. "Transparency builds trust." "Share your journey." "Your audience wants to see the process." I believed all of it. We shared our MRR, our roadmap, our design decisions, our struggles.

Here's what actually happened:

Three competitors replicated our two most distinctive features within 8 weeks of us sharing them publicly. They had bigger teams. They shipped faster.

A prospect in a late-stage deal sent us a screenshot of our "we're still figuring out pricing" post and asked: "Should we be worried about your stability?" We lost the deal.

I spent 6-8 hours per week on content instead of talking to customers. My co-founder spent another 5 hours. That's a combined 11 hours per week — almost 3 full workdays per month — performing instead of building.

Here's my updated take:

Build in public works when you're pre-product and need accountability. It works when your audience IS your customer (creator tools, indie products). And it works when your competitive moat is execution speed, not features.

It does NOT work when you're in a competitive market where features are your differentiation. It does not work when your customers are enterprises who interpret transparency as instability. And it does not work when the founder's time is the primary constraint.

I still share selectively — wins, lessons, frameworks. But I no longer share the roadmap, the revenue, or the struggle.

Not everything that builds audience builds business.

## Why This Post Works

1. **Hook is bold and specific** — "almost killed our startup" is dramatic but earned by the evidence that follows
2. **Acknowledges the counter-argument first** — "I know this is LinkedIn heresy" shows the author understands the dominant view
3. **Three specific pieces of evidence** — competitors copying features, lost deal, time cost. These aren't hypothetical.
4. **Nuanced position** — doesn't say "building in public is always bad." Specifies when it works and when it doesn't. This is the key to a good contrarian post — nuance, not absolutism.
5. **Ends with a memorable line** — "Not everything that builds audience builds business" is quotable and shareable
6. **Practical and experience-based** — every claim comes from the author's direct experience, not theory
7. **No engagement bait** — no "agree?" or "what's your experience?" — the post makes its point and lands
