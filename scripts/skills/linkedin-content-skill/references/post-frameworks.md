# Post Frameworks

8 proven structures for LinkedIn posts. Each includes the structure, when to use it, a full annotated example, and common mistakes.

---

## 1. Insight Post

**Structure:**
1. Hook: State the insight in one provocative or surprising sentence
2. Context: Why does this matter? What's the background?
3. Evidence: A specific example, data point, or story that supports the insight
4. Implication: What should the reader think or do differently?

**When to use:** You've noticed a pattern others haven't articulated. You have a non-obvious observation about your industry.

**Full example:**

```
The best salespeople I know never talk about their product in the first meeting.

I've sat in on 200+ sales calls over the past 3 years as a sales consultant. The top closers (consistently above 140% quota) share one habit: their first call is 80% questions, 20% context-setting. They don't pitch. They diagnose.

The average reps? They pitch within the first 5 minutes. They demo before they understand the problem. They answer questions that weren't asked.

The difference in close rate between these two groups: 38% vs. 12%.

Diagnosis before prescription. It's obvious in medicine. Somehow we forget it in sales.

#sales #b2bsales
```

**Annotation:**
- Hook is a specific, counterintuitive claim (not a vague "here's what I learned")
- Evidence uses real numbers (200+ calls, 140% quota, 38% vs 12%)
- The closing line reframes the insight through analogy (medicine)
- No "agree?" or engagement bait at the end
- Under 800 characters

**Common mistakes:**
- Making the insight too vague ("Good salespeople listen more")
- No evidence — just the assertion without support
- Ending with "What are your tips?" instead of landing the point

## 2. Story Post

**Structure:**
1. Scene-setting: Place the reader in a specific moment (1-2 sentences)
2. Tension: What was the challenge, conflict, or decision?
3. Turn: What happened? What did you do or decide?
4. Resolution: How did it end?
5. Takeaway: What did you learn? Why does it matter beyond your story?

**When to use:** You have a real professional experience with a genuine lesson. The story should be specific enough that only you could tell it.

**Full example:**

```
Three years ago, I fired our biggest client. They were 40% of our revenue.

The account had been growing for 18 months. Great money. But every sprint review turned into a scope negotiation. Every deliverable was met with "can you also..." without additional budget. My team was burning out. Two senior developers told me they were looking elsewhere.

I ran the numbers. We were making 30% less per hour on this client than any other. And the hidden cost — turnover risk, morale damage, opportunity cost of not taking better-fit projects — made it even worse.

So I had the conversation. I told them our working relationship wasn't sustainable at the current terms and proposed a restructured engagement at fair rates. They declined.

We lost 40% of revenue overnight. Worst quarter I've had.

But within 6 months, we'd replaced the revenue with three clients who respected our process. Team retention went to 100% for the next two years. And our profit margin improved by 25%.

The lesson I keep coming back to: revenue that costs you your best people isn't revenue. It's a loan you'll repay at the worst possible time.

#agency #leadership
```

**Annotation:**
- Opens with a specific, bold statement (fired biggest client)
- Real numbers throughout (40%, 30%, 6 months, 100%, 25%)
- Doesn't shy away from the negative (worst quarter)
- Takeaway is a specific, quotable line, not a generic platitude
- Story is clearly real and personal, not a fable

**Common mistakes:**
- Stories that are too long (this is near the max length)
- Stories with no real tension or risk
- Takeaways that are generic ("work-life balance is important")
- Humble bragging disguised as storytelling

## 3. Contrarian Post

**Structure:**
1. The common belief: State it fairly and accurately
2. Your disagreement: Why you see it differently
3. Your alternative: What you believe instead
4. Evidence: Why your view is correct (example, data, experience)
5. Caveat: When the common belief IS correct (shows nuance, not dogma)

**When to use:** You genuinely disagree with a common practice or belief in your industry. Must be backed by real experience, not just contrarianism for clicks.

**Full example:**

```
Unpopular opinion: "Culture fit" is the most overrated hiring criterion in tech.

Every founder I know says culture is their #1 priority. But when I ask them to define their culture, I get vague answers: "We move fast." "We're collaborative." "We're mission-driven."

That's not culture. That's a motivational poster.

When you hire for "culture fit" based on vibes, you hire people who look like you, think like you, and went to the same 5 schools. You get a team that agrees with each other — which feels great until you ship a product that only makes sense to people exactly like you.

What I hire for instead: culture contribution. I want someone who shares our values (integrity, ownership, direct communication) but brings a perspective we don't have.

My best hire last year was someone who argued with me in the interview. She challenged our product roadmap and was right about two of the three points she made.

Culture fit = comfort. Culture contribution = growth.

Caveat: at very early stage (< 5 people), alignment on work style matters a lot. You need people who genuinely enjoy working together 60 hours a week. But once you're past 10 people, optimizing for fit starts to become optimizing for sameness.

#hiring #startups #culture
```

**Annotation:**
- States the common belief fairly (doesn't straw-man it)
- Provides a specific alternative (culture contribution vs culture fit)
- Includes a real example (the hire who argued in the interview)
- Ends with a caveat — this shows intellectual honesty, not just contrarianism
- The closing framework (fit = comfort, contribution = growth) is memorable

**Common mistakes:**
- Being contrarian just for clicks ("Actually, meetings are good")
- Not having evidence for the alternative view
- Straw-manning the common belief
- No caveat — makes you look dogmatic

## 4. How-I Post

**Structure:**
1. The common challenge: Something your audience struggles with
2. Your approach: What you do differently
3. Steps: 3-5 specific, actionable steps (not vague advice)
4. Result: What this produces for you
5. Invitation: "Curious how others handle this" (genuine, not performative)

**Full example:**

```
I write all my client proposals in 45 minutes. Here's the system.

Most consultants spend 3-4 hours per proposal because they start from a blank page every time. I don't.

My system:
1. I keep a master scope library — 40+ pre-written scope blocks for common deliverables. I assemble, I don't write from scratch.
2. Every proposal follows the same 7-section structure. The structure never changes. The content does.
3. I write the pricing table first, then work backwards to justify it. Most people write the scope first and then feel awkward about the price.
4. I timebox to 45 minutes. Whatever isn't done in 45 minutes gets cut — it probably wasn't essential.

This system has generated $1.2M in proposals over the past 18 months. Win rate: 62%.

The insight: your proposal process should have more in common with Lego than with poetry.

#consulting #proposals #freelance
```

**Annotation:**
- Opens with a specific, impressive claim (45 minutes)
- Explains the system in concrete steps, not vague advice
- Each step is specific enough to be actionable
- Includes results (not just process)
- Closing line is memorable (Lego vs poetry)

## 5. Data / Numbers Post

**Structure:**
1. The number: State the surprising statistic
2. Context: Where it comes from, why it's interesting
3. Interpretation: What it means (your analysis, not just the raw data)
4. Implication: What to do about it or what it signals

**Full example:**

```
73% of B2B buyers say they prefer a rep-free buying experience.

Gartner published this. Most sales leaders I've shared it with react defensively: "Our buyers are different."

They're not.

What this number actually means: buyers don't want to avoid salespeople. They want to avoid bad salespeople — the ones who pitch before understanding, who add friction instead of removing it, who make the buying process about their quota rather than the buyer's problem.

The 27% who prefer working with reps? They've worked with good ones. Reps who act as consultants, not pitchers.

The takeaway isn't "fire your sales team." It's "make your sales team the 27% reason, not the 73% problem."

#b2bsales #buyerexperience
```

## 6. Mistake / Lesson Post

**Structure:**
1. The mistake: Be specific and honest
2. The consequence: What went wrong
3. The lesson: What you learned
4. What you do differently now: Concrete change

**Full example:**

```
I once sent a proposal with another client's name in it. The wrong company name. In the executive summary.

The prospect replied: "We're excited about the opportunity too, though we go by [Correct Name], not [Wrong Name]."

I wanted to disappear.

What I learned isn't "proofread your proposals" — though yes, obviously. What I learned is that rushing is the single most expensive habit in consulting. That proposal was the third one I'd written that day. I was optimizing for speed, not quality.

Now I have a rule: one proposal per day, maximum. And every proposal sits for 24 hours before sending. The 24-hour buffer has caught errors, improved pricing, and saved me from sending three proposals I later realized were underpriced.

Slow is expensive until it isn't.

#consulting #lessons
```

## 7. Curated Observation Post

**Structure:**
1. What you observed: A specific thing you saw, read, or experienced
2. Your take: What you think about it
3. Why it matters: Connection to a broader trend or principle
4. Question or forward look: What this might lead to

**Full example:**

```
Noticed something interesting at three pitch meetings this month.

Every prospective client opened with the same question: "How do you use AI in your work?"

Not "What's your approach?" Not "Show me your portfolio." The first question was about AI.

A year ago, nobody asked this. Six months ago, it was one in five. Now it's the opening.

What this tells me: AI competency is becoming a baseline expectation, not a differentiator. Saying "we use AI" is like saying "we use email." The differentiator is HOW and WHERE — which tasks you automate, which you keep human, and how you've redesigned your process around the tools.

If you're a consultant or agency and you haven't clearly articulated your AI approach, you're already behind in the pitch before you start talking about your actual expertise.

#consulting #ai #agencies
```

## 8. Framework / Mental Model Post

**Structure:**
1. Name: Give your framework a memorable name
2. Problem: What problem does it solve?
3. How it works: 3-4 components or steps
4. Example: Show it in action
5. Limitation: When NOT to use it

**Full example:**

```
I use a framework called the "Regret Minimization Filter" for every business decision over $5,000.

Here's how it works:

Three questions:
1. If this goes wrong, will I regret it in 5 years? (Tests downside tolerance)
2. If I don't do this and it would have worked, will I regret it in 5 years? (Tests opportunity cost)
3. Can I reverse this decision within 6 months if it's not working? (Tests reversibility)

If the answers are No/Yes/Yes — do it. The downside is tolerable, the opportunity cost of not doing it is real, and you can course-correct.

If the answers are Yes/No/No — don't. High downside, low opportunity cost, irreversible.

I used this last quarter when deciding whether to hire a full-time developer vs. continuing with freelancers. Answers: No (if it doesn't work, I can let them go) / Yes (I've been limited by freelancer availability for 6 months) / Yes (3-month probation). Did it. Best decision this year.

The limitation: this doesn't work for decisions with asymmetric information. If you don't know enough to answer the questions honestly, the framework gives you false confidence. In those cases, buy more information first.

#decisionmaking #frameworks
```
