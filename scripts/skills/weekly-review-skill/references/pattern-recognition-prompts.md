# Pattern Recognition Prompts

Questions designed to surface recurring patterns across weeks. These are not for every review — use them when someone is stuck, frustrated, or dealing with the same problems repeatedly.

---

## When to Use Pattern Recognition

Use these prompts when:
- A challenge from this week also appeared last week (or the week before)
- Someone says "I keep..." or "This always happens..." or "Every time..."
- The energy audit reveals a chronic drain, not a one-time event
- The same task keeps appearing on the "carry over" list
- Someone can't explain why they feel stuck or dissatisfied

Don't use these every week. They're heavy. Deploy them when a pattern needs breaking.

---

## Category 1: Recurring Frustration Patterns

These questions surface problems that have become invisible because they're so familiar.

### The "Again?" Test
"What frustrated you this week that also frustrated you last week? And the week before?"

Follow-up: "How long has this been going on?" (Most people underestimate. "A few weeks" is usually months.)

### The Groundhog Day Question
"If next week plays out exactly like this week, how would you feel?"

If the answer is "fine" — they're in a sustainable rhythm. If the answer is dread — there's a pattern to break.

### The Invisible Tax
"What recurring task or obligation costs you time every week but never appears on your to-do list?"

Examples: context-switching between tools, unclear handoffs that require follow-up, recurring meetings you attend but don't participate in, emotional labor from managing a difficult colleague.

Follow-up: "What's the total cost of this per month? Is that a cost you're choosing, or one you're tolerating?"

### The Complaint Audit
"What do you complain about most frequently — to friends, to your partner, to yourself?"

Follow-up: "Have you taken any action on this, or have you been venting without changing anything?" (Said gently. The goal is awareness, not judgment.)

---

## Category 2: Avoidance Patterns

These questions reveal what someone is dodging and why. Chronic avoidance is one of the clearest signals that something needs to change.

### The Procrastination Map
"What task did you avoid this week? Not forget — actively avoided. What did you do instead?"

Follow-up: "What's the feeling you get when you think about starting that task?" (Common answers: overwhelm, boredom, fear of failure, fear of conflict.)

Follow-up: "What's the smallest possible first step on that task? Something you could do in 10 minutes."

### The Someday List
"What have you been meaning to do for more than a month?"

Follow-up: "Is this genuinely important, or is it guilt you're carrying? If you decided to never do it, what would happen?"

If it's genuinely important: "What's blocking you? Is it time, clarity, energy, or fear?"
If it's guilt: "Permission granted to cross it off. Not everything deserves to be done."

### The Delegate Dodge
"Is there anything on your plate that someone else should be doing? Why is it still on yours?"

Common answers and their patterns:
- "It's faster if I do it myself" — Short-term efficiency, long-term bottleneck. You're creating a dependency.
- "I don't trust them to do it right" — Control issue. What's the real risk if they do it at 80% of your standard?
- "I haven't asked" — Boundary issue. The ask costs 5 minutes. The carry costs hours.
- "There's no one to delegate to" — Resource issue. Is this a case for hiring, outsourcing, or dropping the task entirely?

### The Conversation Avoidance
"Is there a conversation you've been putting off?"

Follow-up: "What's the worst realistic outcome of having that conversation? Not the catastrophic fantasy — what would actually happen?"

Follow-up: "What's the cost of continuing to avoid it?"

## Category 3: Energy Drain Patterns

These questions identify chronic leaks in energy that week-by-week reviews might miss.

### The Context Switch Count
"How many times did you switch between completely different types of work today?"

Follow-up: "What does a typical day look like? Walk me through the transitions."

Pattern: If someone switches more than 4-5 times per day between deep work, meetings, admin, and reactive tasks, they're losing 20-40% of their productive capacity to switching costs.

Action: Batch similar tasks together. One email block, one meeting block, one deep work block.

### The After-Hours Bleed
"How much work leaked into your evenings or weekends this week?"

Follow-up: "Was that by choice or because the day didn't have enough space?"

Pattern: If after-hours work is chronic (not seasonal or deadline-driven), the day is overcommitted. The solution isn't "better discipline" — it's fewer commitments.

### The Energy Vampire Audit
"Is there a person, meeting, or recurring obligation that consistently leaves you more drained after than before?"

Follow-up: "What specifically about it drains you? Is it the content, the format, or the person?"

Action categories:
- **Fixable:** Change the format, shorten the meeting, set an agenda, change your role in it
- **Avoidable:** Delegate attendance, decline, or renegotiate your involvement
- **Tolerable:** Accept it as a cost of the role, but schedule recovery time after it

### The Sunday Dread Test
"When you think about Monday, what's the first feeling? Dread, neutral, or anticipation?"

- **Dread:** Something specific is wrong. Dig into what. Is it one thing or the general shape of the work?
- **Neutral:** Sustainable. Not thriving, not suffering.
- **Anticipation:** This is the target state. What's creating it? Protect those elements.

## Category 4: Decision Avoidance Patterns

These questions surface decisions being deferred, which often masquerade as "being busy."

### The Pending Decision Inventory
"What decisions are you sitting on right now? Things you haven't decided but need to."

Follow-up: "For each one — what information are you waiting for, or are you waiting because the decision is uncomfortable?"

Pattern: Most pending decisions are pending not because of missing information but because of fear of commitment. The information they're "waiting for" will never be conclusive enough to make the decision feel safe.

Action: "What would you decide if you had to decide by Friday? Go with that. Perfectionism in decisions costs more than an imperfect choice."

### The Reversibility Check
"Which of your pending decisions are reversible — you can change your mind later — and which are one-way doors?"

For reversible decisions: "Make them faster. You can adjust later. The cost of delay is higher than the cost of being wrong."

For irreversible decisions: "These deserve more deliberation. But even here, set a deadline. Decide by [date], and stop collecting information after [earlier date]."

### The Consensus Trap
"Are you waiting for someone else's input before deciding? Do you actually need it, or are you sharing the risk?"

Pattern: Some people seek consensus not for better decisions but to distribute blame if things go wrong. If you notice this pattern: "You already know what the right call is. Make it."

## Category 5: Growth and Fulfillment Patterns

These questions look beyond productivity to whether the work itself is aligned with what the person wants.

### The 80/20 Fulfillment Check
"What percentage of your work this week was genuinely fulfilling versus just necessary?"

If below 20%: "That's unsustainable long-term. What would a week look like where 40% of your time was work you find meaningful?"

### The Strengths Alignment
"What tasks this week used your best skills? What tasks made you feel like you were underutilizing yourself?"

Pattern: If someone consistently feels underutilized, they're either in the wrong role, or they've been promoted into management when they should be doing the work.

### The Three-Year Question
"Is what you're doing this week moving you toward where you want to be in three years?"

This question cuts through weekly noise. It's not about this week being perfect — it's about direction. If someone answers "no" for several weeks in a row, the weekly review has surfaced something bigger than task management.

### The Identity Check
"Are you becoming the person you want to be through this work? Or is the work shaping you into something you don't want?"

This is a heavy question. Don't use it every week. Use it when someone seems fundamentally misaligned — doing good work but feeling empty.

---

## How to Introduce Pattern Prompts

Don't frame these as diagnostic. Frame them as curious.

**Good:** "I noticed you mentioned context-switching again. Can I ask you something? How many times did you switch between different types of work today?"

**Bad:** "Let's do a pattern analysis of your recurring issues."

The goal is insight, not therapy. If a pattern becomes clear, name it simply: "It sounds like a boundary issue." or "This seems like an energy management problem more than a time management problem." Then move to the priority-setting methods to address it.
