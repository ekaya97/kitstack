# Priority-Setting Methods

Five methods for deciding what matters next week. The right method depends on the person's problem: too many things (Eisenhower, Top 3), wrong things (MoSCoW), no energy (Energy Management), no focus (Weekly Theming).

---

## 1. Eisenhower Matrix

**Best for:** People who are busy but don't feel productive. The "I'm always putting out fires" person.

**The matrix:**

|                    | **Urgent**              | **Not Urgent**               |
|--------------------|-------------------------|------------------------------|
| **Important**      | DO FIRST: Crisis, deadlines, critical client issues | SCHEDULE: Strategy, skill-building, relationship-building, prevention |
| **Not Important**  | DELEGATE: Most emails, some meetings, minor requests | DROP: Time-wasters, busywork, low-value habits |

**How to use in a weekly review:**
1. List everything on the plate for next week
2. Categorize each item into a quadrant
3. The review should reveal that most "urgent" tasks are actually Quadrant 3 (urgent but not important) — these are the delegation targets
4. Quadrant 2 (important but not urgent) is where long-term value lives — protect time for these

**Common traps:**
- **Everything feels urgent.** If everything is Q1, you have a planning problem, not a prioritization problem. Ask: "What happens if this waits 3 days?" If the answer is "nothing much," it's not truly urgent.
- **Guilt about Q4.** People feel bad about dropping things. Reframe: "Saying no to this is saying yes to something in Q2."
- **Delegation avoidance.** "It's faster if I do it myself" is a Q3 trap. The 10 minutes you save today costs you hours next month.

**Weekly review application:**
After listing next week's priorities, ask: "Which quadrant does each one fall in?" Then:
- Q1 items: These are your non-negotiables. Schedule them first.
- Q2 items: Block time for at least one. This is your investment in next month.
- Q3 items: Who else could handle these? Can you push back on the deadline?
- Q4 items: Cross them off. Right now. Don't carry them to next week.

## 2. MoSCoW Method

**Best for:** People managing projects with competing demands. Product managers, consultants, anyone negotiating scope.

**Categories:**
- **Must have:** Non-negotiable. If this doesn't happen, the week is a failure.
- **Should have:** Important but not critical. Plan for it, but it can slip without disaster.
- **Could have:** Nice to have. Do it if everything else is done.
- **Won't have (this week):** Explicitly excluded. Not forgotten — deferred with intention.

**How to use in a weekly review:**
1. List all tasks and commitments for next week
2. Force-rank into M/S/C/W
3. Rule: Maximum 2 Must Haves. If you have 5 Must Haves, you haven't prioritized — you've just listed things.
4. The "Won't Have" list is the most valuable output. It gives you permission to not do things.

**The "Won't Have" power move:**
Most people resist putting things in Won't Have. They feel like they're failing. Reframe: "Won't Have this week doesn't mean never. It means not now. You're choosing to do fewer things better."

Send the Won't Have list to anyone affected. "I'm focusing on X and Y this week. Z is deferred to next week." This prevents surprises and manages expectations.

**Example:**
| Category | Item |
|----------|------|
| Must | Ship the API integration (client deadline Friday) |
| Must | Finalize the hiring decision for the senior role |
| Should | Review the Q3 marketing plan |
| Should | Prep the board deck for next week's meeting |
| Could | Write the blog post on our new feature |
| Won't | Attend the industry conference (sending a team member instead) |
| Won't | Redesign the internal dashboard (moving to sprint 7) |

## 3. Top 3 Method

**Best for:** Overwhelmed people who need radical simplification. The "I have a list of 47 things" person.

**The rule:** You have exactly 3 priorities for the week. Not 5. Not 7. Three.

**How to identify the 3:**
1. Ask: "If I could only accomplish 3 things this week and everything else stayed undone, which 3 would I pick?"
2. For each of the 3, define "done" — a concrete, observable outcome
3. Everything else goes on a "Not This Week" list

**Why 3, not 5:**
- Research on cognitive load suggests humans can effectively juggle 3-4 priorities before quality degrades
- 3 is achievable in a week even with unexpected interruptions
- 3 forces trade-offs, which forces clarity
- Accomplishing 3 things fully is more valuable than half-finishing 7

**The daily cascade:**
Once you have your Top 3 for the week, each morning ask: "Which of these 3 gets my best energy today?" Start there. Non-Top-3 tasks only happen after you've advanced at least one of the 3.

**Weekly review application:**
At the end of the review, after discussing priorities, force the compression: "Okay. Of everything we discussed, give me exactly 3. These are the ones we'll check against next week."

If they resist: "I know there are more than 3 things on your plate. But which 3, if done well, would make everything else easier or less necessary?"

**Example:**
1. **Close the Acme deal** — Done when: Signed contract and first invoice sent
2. **Ship v2.1 to production** — Done when: Live, no P0 bugs in first 24 hours
3. **Hire the marketing lead** — Done when: Offer accepted

Everything else is context. These 3 are the week.

## 4. Energy Management Method

**Best for:** People who plan well but can't execute because they're depleted. The "I know what to do but I can't make myself do it" person.

**Principle:** Instead of organizing tasks by importance or deadline, organize them by energy requirement and match them to your energy availability.

**Energy mapping:**

### Step 1: Map your energy curve
Everyone has predictable energy patterns across the day and week. Identify yours:

| Time block | Typical energy level | Best for |
|-----------|---------------------|----------|
| Early morning (6-9am) | Rising | Creative work, strategic thinking, writing |
| Late morning (9-12pm) | Peak | Hardest task of the day, deep work, critical decisions |
| Early afternoon (1-3pm) | Post-lunch dip | Administrative tasks, emails, routine meetings |
| Late afternoon (3-5pm) | Second wind (for some) or decline | Collaborative work, calls, lighter tasks |
| Evening (after 5pm) | Varies | Planning tomorrow, light reading, shutting down |

### Step 2: Categorize tasks by energy cost

| Category | Description | Examples |
|----------|-------------|---------|
| **High energy** | Requires deep focus, creativity, or emotional labor | Writing proposals, strategic planning, difficult conversations, coding complex features |
| **Medium energy** | Requires attention but not peak cognitive load | Client calls, code reviews, project planning, hiring interviews |
| **Low energy** | Can do on autopilot | Email, invoicing, scheduling, updating project boards, data entry |

### Step 3: Match tasks to energy slots

Rule: Never schedule a high-energy task during a low-energy time. It won't get done, and you'll feel like a failure for "procrastinating."

**Weekly review application:**
For each of next week's priorities, ask:
- "Is this a high, medium, or low energy task?"
- "When in the day will you have that level of energy?"
- "What day of the week are you most likely to have the capacity for this?"

Then: "Block the time. Put your highest-energy task in your highest-energy slot. Protect it like a meeting with your most important client."

### Step 4: Energy recovery planning

| Recovery type | When to use | Examples |
|--------------|-------------|---------|
| Micro-recovery (5-15 min) | Between tasks | Walk, stretch, coffee without phone, stare out window |
| Transition break (15-30 min) | Between deep work and meetings | Change location, light reading, snack |
| Recovery block (1-2 hours) | After draining events | Post-presentation, after difficult conversations |
| Recovery day | After sprints or travel | Light admin only, no deep work, no calls |

## 5. Weekly Theming

**Best for:** People who context-switch too much. The "every day feels different and I never make progress on anything" person.

**Principle:** Assign a theme to each day of the week. All tasks of that type go on that day. Other days, that type of work doesn't exist.

**Example theme structure:**

| Day | Theme | Activities |
|-----|-------|-----------|
| Monday | Admin & Planning | Emails, invoices, project updates, weekly planning |
| Tuesday | Deep Work | Primary project focus, no meetings, no email |
| Wednesday | Clients & Meetings | All calls, meetings, client reviews batched here |
| Thursday | Deep Work | Secondary project, writing, creative work |
| Friday | Review & Outreach | Weekly review, business development, networking |

**Why it works:**
- Eliminates the "What should I be doing right now?" question — the day's theme decides
- Reduces context-switching cost (which research estimates at 20-40% of productive time)
- Creates permission to ignore certain types of work on certain days
- Clients and colleagues learn your rhythm: "She does client calls on Wednesday"

**How to implement gradually:**
- Week 1: Theme just one day (e.g., Tuesday = No Meetings)
- Week 2: Theme two days
- Week 3: Full weekly theme
- It takes 2-3 weeks for others to learn and respect your rhythm

**Weekly review application:**
During the priorities phase, ask: "Which day does each priority live on? If a priority doesn't have a clear day, it's at risk of never happening."

If someone resists: "You don't have to theme every day. Start with one protected day. Which type of work suffers most from interruptions?" That day gets themed first.

**Common themes by role:**

| Role | Common themes |
|------|--------------|
| Freelancer | Client Work / Business Dev / Admin / Creative / Buffer |
| Founder | Product / Sales / Team / Strategy / Admin |
| Team Lead | 1:1s & Team / Deep Work / Planning / Cross-functional / Buffer |
| Content Creator | Research / Writing / Editing & Publishing / Engagement / Business |

## Method Selection Guide

| Symptom | Method |
|---------|--------|
| "I'm always busy but not productive" | Eisenhower Matrix |
| "I have too many competing priorities" | MoSCoW |
| "I have too much on my plate" | Top 3 |
| "I know what to do but can't make myself do it" | Energy Management |
| "Every day is different and I never finish anything" | Weekly Theming |
| "I don't know what matters" | Top 3 (the constraint forces clarity) |
| "I'm burned out" | Energy Management + Weekly Theming (recover first, then structure) |

## Combining Methods

The most effective combination:
1. **Top 3** to identify the week's priorities
2. **Energy Management** to place them in the right time slots
3. **Weekly Theming** to protect the blocks from interruption

Don't use all five methods at once. Pick the one that addresses the user's primary symptom.
