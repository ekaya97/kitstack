# Reflection Frameworks

Five structured frameworks for weekly reflection. Each has a different strength. Use the one that fits the person's situation and mood.

---

## 1. Start / Stop / Continue

**Best for:** General weekly reviews. Works for any role. Good default.

**Structure:**
- **Start:** What should I begin doing that I'm not doing yet?
- **Stop:** What should I stop doing because it's not serving me?
- **Continue:** What's working that I should keep doing?

**When to use:**
- First-time reviewers (simple, intuitive)
- When someone feels stuck in routine and needs to shake things up
- When energy is low and a complex framework would feel like work

**How to facilitate:**
1. Start with "Continue" — it's easier and builds momentum. "What's working right now that you want to protect?"
2. Then "Stop" — "What are you doing that doesn't deserve your time anymore?"
3. End with "Start" — "What's one thing you've been meaning to do that would make a real difference?"

**Watch out for:**
- People loading up the "Start" list. Push back: "You can only add a Start if you also add a Stop. Capacity is finite."
- Vague items like "be more productive." Demand specifics: "What does that look like at 9am Monday?"

**Example output:**
| Category | Item | Why |
|----------|------|-----|
| Start | Block 2 hours Monday morning for deep work | I keep losing mornings to Slack and email |
| Stop | Attending the Thursday all-hands (I never contribute and it breaks my flow) | Sunk cost — I go because I've always gone |
| Continue | Daily standup notes to the team | Forces me to articulate what matters each day |

## 2. Plus / Delta

**Best for:** Performance-oriented reviews. Good for people who resist "touchy-feely" frameworks.

**Structure:**
- **Plus (+):** What went well? What should we keep?
- **Delta (triangle):** What should change? (Not "minus" — the reframe matters.)

**When to use:**
- People who are uncomfortable with the word "failure" or "weakness"
- Teams doing agile retrospectives
- When you want to focus on forward momentum, not dwelling on negatives

**How to facilitate:**
1. List plusses first. Be specific — not "good week" but "closed the deal with Acme after 3 months."
2. For deltas, frame as change requests, not complaints. Not "meetings are too long" but "change: cap meetings at 30 minutes and require an agenda."
3. For each delta, immediately ask: "What would the first step to change this look like?"

**Why Delta, not Minus:**
The word "minus" or "negative" puts people in a defensive posture. "Delta" (change) is forward-looking. It assumes the situation can be improved. This isn't just semantics — it changes how people answer. "What was bad?" produces complaints. "What would you change?" produces solutions.

**Example output:**
| Plus (+) | Delta (change) |
|----------|----------------|
| Shipped the v2.1 release on time | Change: Start QA 2 days earlier — we barely made it |
| New hire is ramping fast | Change: Create a proper onboarding doc so the next hire doesn't rely on tribal knowledge |
| Client feedback on the prototype was positive | Change: Record client calls — I forgot half the feedback by the next day |

## 3. 5 Whys (Root Cause Reflection)

**Best for:** Digging into a specific recurring problem. Not for a general review — use when one issue dominated the week.

**Structure:**
1. State the problem
2. Ask "Why did this happen?"
3. Take the answer and ask "Why?" again
4. Repeat until you reach a root cause (usually 3-5 levels deep)

**When to use:**
- A challenge from the review keeps recurring week after week
- Someone says "I don't know why this keeps happening"
- When surface-level fixes haven't worked (e.g., "I just need to be more disciplined" — no, you need to understand WHY discipline breaks down)

**How to facilitate:**
1. Start: "Let's dig into [the problem]. Why did this happen?"
2. After each answer, gently push: "And why is that the case?"
3. Stop when you hit something they can actually change. The root cause is actionable.
4. If they get defensive, soften: "This isn't about blame — it's about finding the root so you can fix it once."

**Example chain:**

| Level | Question | Answer |
|-------|----------|--------|
| 1 | Why did the project slip by a week? | Because the client review took 8 days instead of 3 |
| 2 | Why did the client review take so long? | Because I sent it to the project manager and they had to get 4 people to approve |
| 3 | Why did approval require 4 people? | Because I never asked who the decision-maker was — I assumed it was the PM |
| 4 | Why didn't I ask who the decision-maker is? | Because I don't have that question in my kickoff process |
| **Root cause** | **Action:** Add "Who has final approval?" to the project kickoff checklist | |

**Watch out for:**
- Going too deep into things outside their control. Stop at the level where they can act.
- Using this for every problem. It's heavy. Reserve it for recurring issues.
- Confusing "5 Whys" with interrogation. The tone should be curious, not prosecutorial.

## 4. Rose / Thorn / Bud

**Best for:** Creative thinkers. People who respond better to metaphor than frameworks. Good for visual and emotional processors.

**Structure:**
- **Rose:** Something that went well, a highlight, a moment of joy or pride
- **Thorn:** A pain point, a challenge, something that hurt
- **Bud:** Something with potential — an idea, an opportunity, something you're curious about

**When to use:**
- People who find Start/Stop/Continue too corporate
- When you want to add a future-looking element (the Bud)
- When someone is creative or emotional in their processing style
- When the week had a mix of good and bad and you need to honor both

**How to facilitate:**
1. "What was your rose this week — the highlight?" (Let them savor it.)
2. "And the thorn — what pricked you?" (Let them vent. Don't rush to solve.)
3. "What's your bud — something that's starting to grow, something you're curious about?" (This is the optimism injector.)

**Why the Bud matters:**
Most reflection frameworks are backward-looking. The Bud introduces possibility. It asks: "What's emerging?" This is particularly valuable for founders and creators who need to stay attuned to new opportunities, not just optimize current operations.

**Example output:**
- **Rose:** Had a 2-hour deep work block on Wednesday that produced the entire product spec. Reminded me what I'm capable of when I'm not in meetings.
- **Thorn:** The sales call on Thursday. I talked too much and didn't listen. Lost the deal. Need to practice the 60/40 listening rule.
- **Bud:** A customer mentioned they're using our product for a use case I never considered — fleet management. Worth exploring.

## 5. The 4 Ls (Liked / Learned / Lacked / Longed For)

**Best for:** Comprehensive reviews that go beyond tasks and into fulfillment. Good for people questioning whether they're on the right path.

**Structure:**
- **Liked:** What did I enjoy? What tasks or moments made me feel engaged?
- **Learned:** What new knowledge, skill, or insight did I gain?
- **Lacked:** What was missing? Resources, support, information, time, clarity?
- **Longed For:** What do I wish had happened? What would my ideal version of this week look like?

**When to use:**
- Quarterly reviews or when someone wants a deeper check-in than usual
- People considering career changes, role shifts, or major decisions
- When the weekly review keeps surfacing the same dissatisfaction
- When someone says "I'm fine" but you sense they're not

**How to facilitate:**
1. "What did you genuinely enjoy this week? Not what you accomplished — what you liked doing."
2. "What did you learn? Could be about the work, about people, about yourself."
3. "What was missing? What would have made this week better if you'd had it?"
4. "If you could redesign this week from scratch, what would your ideal version look like?"

**Why "Longed For" is the most powerful question:**
Most reflection frameworks ask "what went wrong?" The Longed For question asks "what does right look like?" This is generative. It surfaces not just problems but desires — and desires are better motivators than problems. When someone says "I longed for a day without meetings," that's a clearer signal than "I had too many meetings."

**Example output:**
- **Liked:** Pair-programming session with the junior dev. Teaching forces me to clarify my own thinking.
- **Learned:** Our biggest customer is evaluating competitors. Need to shift from feature work to retention.
- **Lacked:** Clear priorities from leadership. I spent Monday guessing what mattered most.
- **Longed For:** A week where I could ship one thing from start to finish without interruption. Everything this week was half-done.

## Framework Selection Guide

| Situation | Recommended Framework |
|-----------|----------------------|
| First review, not sure what format | Start / Stop / Continue |
| Performance-focused, analytical person | Plus / Delta |
| One specific problem keeps recurring | 5 Whys |
| Creative thinker, wants metaphor | Rose / Thorn / Bud |
| Deep reflection, questioning direction | 4 Ls |
| Quick review (5 minutes) | Rose / Thorn only |
| Team retrospective | Plus / Delta or Start / Stop / Continue |
| Burnout suspected | 4 Ls (the Longed For question is diagnostic) |

## Combining Frameworks

For a thorough review, you can combine:
1. Start with **Rose / Thorn / Bud** for the emotional overview
2. Use **5 Whys** to drill into the Thorn if it's recurring
3. End with **Start / Stop / Continue** for concrete next-week actions

Don't combine more than two in a single session. Framework fatigue is real.
