# US & UK Contract Law Basics

Key concepts for reviewing contracts governed by US or UK law. Focused on the differences from German law that affect risk assessment for freelancers and service providers.

---

## 1. US Contract Law — Core Principles

### Freedom of Contract
US law provides significantly more freedom of contract than German law. There is no equivalent to the German AGB-Recht (SS 305-310 BGB) for B2B contracts. Courts generally enforce terms as written, even if they're unfavorable to one party.

**Implication for contract review:** One-sided clauses that would be void under German AGB law may be fully enforceable under US law. Take US contracts at face value — assume every clause will be enforced.

### Consideration
US contracts require "consideration" — something of value exchanged by both parties. This is typically: services for payment. Unlike German law, a one-sided promise (without consideration) is generally not enforceable.

### State-by-State Variation
US contract law varies by state. Key differences:

**California:**
- Non-compete agreements are void (Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code SS 16600)
- Strong employee/contractor protections (ABC test for classification)
- Implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing

**New York:**
- Non-competes enforceable if "reasonable" (scope, duration, geography)
- Strong enforcement of written contracts (Statute of Frauds)
- Sophisticated commercial parties held to higher standard

**Texas:**
- Non-competes enforceable if reasonable and supported by consideration
- Relatively employer-friendly
- Mandatory arbitration widely enforced

**Delaware:**
- Preferred jurisdiction for corporate agreements
- Business-friendly courts (Court of Chancery)
- Non-competes enforceable if reasonable

### Choice of Law
US contracts often specify governing state law. When reviewing:
- Check which state's law governs
- Check where disputes must be resolved (could be a different state)
- If you're based outside the US, consider whether the chosen jurisdiction is practical for you

## 2. US-Specific Red Flags

### Independent Contractor Classification
The IRS and state agencies use multi-factor tests to distinguish contractors from employees. Key factors:

**RED flags for misclassification:**
- Contract specifies work hours, location, or methods
- Client provides equipment and tools
- Contractor can only work for one client
- Ongoing relationship without project scope
- Contractor is paid a regular salary (not per project)

**Consequences of misclassification:** Back taxes (employer's share of FICA), penalties, benefits liability. The client bears most risk, but the contractor may also face tax consequences.

**IRS 20-Factor Test (simplified to key factors):**
1. Behavioral control — does the client dictate how work is performed?
2. Financial control — does the contractor have opportunity for profit/loss?
3. Relationship — is there a written contract? Are benefits provided?

### "Work Made for Hire" Doctrine
Under US copyright law (17 U.S.C. SS 101), a "work made for hire" belongs to the commissioning party from inception — the creator never owns it.

**For independent contractors, "work for hire" only applies to 9 specific categories:**
1. Contribution to a collective work
2. Part of a motion picture or audiovisual work
3. Translation
4. Supplementary work
5. Compilation
6. Instructional text
7. Test or test answer
8. Atlas
9. Sound recording (added 1999)

**Most freelance work (software, designs, marketing materials) does NOT qualify as work for hire.** If the contract says "work for hire" for work that doesn't fit these categories, the clause may be ineffective — but the intent to transfer ownership may still be enforceable through the assignment clause.

**What to look for:** Contracts often use "work made for hire, and to the extent it is not, Contractor hereby assigns all rights." This belt-and-suspenders approach is standard and generally enforceable.

### At-Will Engagement
Most US contractor agreements can be terminated by either party at any time, for any reason (similar to at-will employment). This is standard but should include:
- Payment for work completed
- Reasonable notice period
- Return of materials/access

### Arbitration Clauses
Very common in US contracts and generally enforceable. Key considerations:
- **Mandatory vs. optional:** Mandatory arbitration means you cannot go to court
- **Arbitration body:** AAA (American Arbitration Association) and JAMS are standard. Custom/unknown arbitration bodies are a red flag.
- **Cost allocation:** Arbitration filing fees can be $3,000-15,000. Check who pays. Standard: split evenly, or loser pays.
- **Class action waiver:** Often bundled with arbitration. Prevents joining class actions.
- **Location:** Arbitration in a distant city is expensive and burdensome.

### Attorney's Fees
US follows the "American Rule" — each party pays its own legal fees unless the contract states otherwise. Many contracts include: "The prevailing party shall be entitled to recover reasonable attorney's fees." This clause is double-edged: it helps you if you win but hurts you if you lose.

## 3. UK Contract Law — Core Principles

### Common Law + Statutory Protections
UK contract law is common law (judge-made) supplemented by statutes:
- **Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977 (UCTA):** Limits exclusion/limitation of liability. Cannot exclude liability for negligence causing death/personal injury. B2B limitation clauses must be "reasonable."
- **Consumer Rights Act 2015:** Replaces UCTA for consumer contracts. Not relevant for most freelance B2B contracts.
- **Late Payment of Commercial Debts (Interest) Act 1998:** Automatic right to interest on late payments (8% + Bank of England base rate) and compensation (GBP 40-100 depending on debt size).

### IR35 — Off-Payroll Working Rules
UK equivalent of Scheinselbststandigkeit. Determines whether a contractor is "inside IR35" (effectively employed) or "outside IR35" (genuinely self-employed).

**Key factors:**
- **Mutuality of obligation:** Is the client obligated to offer work and the contractor obligated to accept?
- **Control:** Does the client control what, how, when, and where the work is done?
- **Substitution:** Can the contractor send a substitute to do the work?
- **Financial risk:** Does the contractor bear financial risk and opportunity for profit?
- **Integration:** Is the contractor integrated into the client's organization?

**Since April 2021:** Medium/large clients must determine IR35 status and bear the tax risk. The determination must be communicated to the contractor. Contractors can challenge the determination.

**In contracts, look for:** "IR35 assessment" or "status determination statement" references. If absent, the contractor may bear the risk.

### Implied Terms
UK law implies certain terms into contracts:
- Duty to perform with reasonable care and skill (Supply of Goods and Services Act 1982)
- Payment within a reasonable time if not specified
- Duty of good faith (increasingly recognized in long-term commercial contracts)

## 4. Key Differences: German vs. US vs. UK

| Topic | Germany (BGB) | US (Common Law) | UK (Common Law) |
|-------|--------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| **Standard terms control** | Strong (SS 305-310 BGB) | Minimal for B2B | Moderate (UCTA) |
| **Unfair clauses** | Automatically void | Generally enforceable | Must be "reasonable" |
| **Non-competes** | Need compensation (Karenzentschadigung) | State-dependent (void in CA) | Must be reasonable |
| **IP transfer** | Copyright non-transferable; license usage rights | Assignable; work-for-hire doctrine | Assignable; no work-for-hire |
| **Late payment** | SS 288 BGB: 9% above base rate (B2B) | No statutory interest (contract-dependent) | 8% + BoE base rate + GBP 40-100 |
| **Termination (Werkvertrag)** | SS 648: client can terminate anytime, must pay | At-will unless contract says otherwise | Reasonable notice implied |
| **Liability exclusion** | Cannot exclude for Vorsatz/grobe Fahrlassigkeit | Broadly enforceable | Cannot exclude for negligence (death/injury) |
| **Contractor classification** | Scheinselbststandigkeit (Deutsche Rentenversicherung) | IRS 20-factor test | IR35 |
| **Dispute resolution** | Ordinary courts; arbitration if agreed | Arbitration very common | Courts or arbitration |
| **Contract language** | German preferred; English enforceable | English | English |

## 5. Cross-Border Contract Considerations

When a contract involves parties in different jurisdictions:

### Which Law Applies?
- Check the "Governing Law" clause — this is critical
- If no governing law specified, it defaults to complex conflict-of-laws rules (Rome I Regulation in EU)
- As a general rule: the law of the party who drafted the contract typically governs (because they chose it)

### Jurisdiction vs. Governing Law
These are different:
- **Governing law:** Which country's laws interpret the contract
- **Jurisdiction:** Which country's courts handle disputes

**Example of a problematic combination:** Governing law: State of Delaware. Jurisdiction: Courts of London. This means a German freelancer would need to sue in London under Delaware law.

### Enforcement
- Within EU: Brussels I Regulation — judgments enforceable across member states
- EU-US: No automatic enforcement. Must sue again in the other jurisdiction.
- EU-UK (post-Brexit): Hague Convention may apply; enforcement is more complex than pre-Brexit

### Currency and Tax
- Check payment currency — exchange rate risk falls on the party receiving foreign currency
- Check VAT/sales tax provisions — especially relevant for EU-US contracts (reverse charge mechanism within EU)
- Check withholding tax provisions — some countries withhold tax on payments to foreign contractors

## 6. Practical Checklist for Cross-Border Contracts

When reviewing a contract governed by US or UK law (as a non-US/UK party):

- [ ] **Governing law and jurisdiction identified** — do you understand which laws apply?
- [ ] **Arbitration clause reviewed** — is arbitration mandatory? Where? Who pays?
- [ ] **Payment currency specified** — who bears exchange rate risk?
- [ ] **Tax treatment clear** — withholding tax? VAT? Reverse charge?
- [ ] **Non-compete reviewed against local law** — US non-compete may be unenforceable in your jurisdiction
- [ ] **IP assignment reviewed** — work-for-hire claim valid? Assignment vs. license?
- [ ] **Liability cap proportional** — US contracts often have unlimited liability; negotiate a cap
- [ ] **Insurance requirements feasible** — US contracts may require insurance types unavailable in your country
- [ ] **Dispute location practical** — can you actually travel to and afford proceedings in the specified jurisdiction?
- [ ] **Termination terms reviewed** — at-will termination without kill fee?
