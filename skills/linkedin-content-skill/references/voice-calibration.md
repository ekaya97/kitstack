# Voice Calibration

How to analyze a user's writing voice from samples and produce content that sounds like them, not like a ghostwriter or AI.

---

## Why Voice Calibration Matters

The #1 reason LinkedIn content fails: it doesn't sound like the person posting it. Their connections know their voice. Their audience has expectations. When a post reads like it was written by someone else, engagement drops and trust erodes.

Voice calibration is not about mimicry. It's about understanding the user's natural communication patterns and writing within those patterns while elevating clarity and impact.

---

## Step 1: Collect Samples

Ask the user for 2-5 pieces of their own writing:

**Best sources (in order of preference):**
1. Previous LinkedIn posts they wrote themselves
2. Long-form emails to clients or colleagues
3. Slack messages or chat conversations where they explain something
4. Blog posts or articles
5. Transcripts of talks or podcast appearances

**If they can't provide samples:** Ask them to describe their voice in 2-3 sentences, or ask them to name 2-3 LinkedIn creators whose voice they admire (then calibrate toward that direction while maintaining their own identity).

---

## Step 2: Extract Voice Dimensions

Analyze the samples across these 8 dimensions:

### 1. Sentence Length
- **Short and punchy:** Average 8-12 words. Fragments common. "Works great for founders."
- **Medium and balanced:** Average 12-18 words. Complete sentences. Natural flow.
- **Long and flowing:** Average 18-25 words. Complex structures. Thoughtful cadence.
- **Mixed:** Alternates between short and long deliberately. Uses short sentences for emphasis.

### 2. Vocabulary Level
- **Simple/direct:** Common words, plain language. "We fixed the problem." 
- **Professional/precise:** Industry terminology where appropriate. "We optimized the conversion funnel."
- **Sophisticated/nuanced:** Complex ideas expressed with precision. "The asymmetry between perceived risk and actual exposure created an opportunity."
- **Technical:** Domain-specific jargon used naturally (appropriate when audience is technical).

### 3. Formality
- **Casual:** Contractions, sentence fragments, colloquialisms. "Gonna be real — this didn't work."
- **Conversational:** Natural but grammatically correct. "I tried this approach, and it didn't work."
- **Professional:** Clean, polished, no slang. "The approach failed to produce the expected results."
- **Formal:** Complete sentences, no contractions, structured arguments. (Rare on LinkedIn.)

### 4. Use of Humor
- **Dry wit:** Understated, deadpan. The humor is in the observation, not in trying to be funny.
- **Self-deprecating:** Makes themselves the punchline. "Turns out I was the bottleneck. Plot twist: I'm always the bottleneck."
- **Playful:** Light-hearted, energetic. Not trying to be a comedian, but enjoys language.
- **None:** Serious, authoritative tone. Humor would feel forced.

### 5. Perspective
- **First person narrator:** Heavy use of "I" — personal stories, own experiences. "I noticed..." "I tried..."
- **We/team:** Frames achievements as collective. "We shipped..." "Our team discovered..."
- **Observer/analyst:** Describes patterns from a distance. "The market is shifting..." "Companies that do X..."
- **Direct address:** Talks to the reader. "You've probably noticed..." "If you're a consultant..."

### 6. Emotional Openness
- **Guarded:** Shares insights and data, not feelings. Professional distance maintained.
- **Moderate:** Occasionally shares challenges or frustrations. Keeps emotional content professional.
- **Open:** Freely shares struggles, doubts, and excitement. Authentically vulnerable.
- **Selective:** Opens up about specific topics (failure, growth) but maintains privacy in others.

### 7. Paragraph Structure
- **Tight:** 1-2 sentences per paragraph. Lots of white space. Fast-moving.
- **Standard:** 2-3 sentences per paragraph. Natural reading rhythm.
- **Dense:** 3-4 sentences per paragraph. More like traditional writing.
- **Mixed:** Varies deliberately — short for emphasis, longer for explanation.

### 8. Signature Patterns
Look for idiosyncrasies that make the voice unique:
- Do they use parenthetical asides? (like this)
- Do they use dashes for interruptions — like I'm doing here?
- Do they use specific catchphrases or recurring expressions?
- Do they favor certain metaphor domains (sports, cooking, music)?
- Do they number things? ("Two reasons:")
- Do they use qualifiers ("probably," "might," "I think") or are they declarative?

---

## Step 3: Create Voice Profile

After analysis, summarize the voice profile in this format:

```
## Voice Profile: [User Name]

**Sentence length:** [Short/Medium/Long/Mixed]
**Vocabulary:** [Simple/Professional/Sophisticated/Technical]
**Formality:** [Casual/Conversational/Professional/Formal]
**Humor:** [Dry/Self-deprecating/Playful/None]
**Perspective:** [First person/We/Observer/Direct address]
**Emotional openness:** [Guarded/Moderate/Open/Selective]
**Paragraph structure:** [Tight/Standard/Dense/Mixed]
**Signature patterns:** [List unique patterns]

**Voice summary:** [2-3 sentence description of how they sound]

**Example sentence in their voice:**
"[A sentence you'd write as them]"
```

---

## Step 4: Apply the Voice

When writing content, follow these rules:

1. **Match sentence length distribution.** If they write short, keep it short. Don't suddenly produce flowing prose for a punchy writer.
2. **Use their vocabulary, not yours.** If they say "clients," don't say "stakeholders." If they say "shipped," don't say "delivered the deliverables."
3. **Mirror their formality level.** If they use contractions, use contractions. If they don't, don't.
4. **Don't inject humor if they're not funny.** And don't suppress it if they are.
5. **Stay in their perspective.** If they write "I" posts, don't produce "we" posts.
6. **Respect their emotional boundaries.** If they're guarded, don't write vulnerably. If they're open, don't sanitize.
7. **Use their signature patterns.** If they love parenthetical asides, include some. If they never use lists, avoid lists.

---

## Step 5: Validate

After writing the first post, ask the user:

"Does this sound like you? Anything that feels off — too formal, too casual, wrong vocabulary, not your usual rhythm?"

Adjust based on their feedback. Voice calibration improves with iteration.

---

## Common Calibration Mistakes

1. **Over-polishing.** Making every sentence grammatically perfect when the user writes more casually. Imperfection is part of voice.
2. **Adding humor to a serious person.** If their samples have zero humor, don't add wit.
3. **Inflating vocabulary.** Using $10 words when the user uses $1 words.
4. **Standardizing paragraph length.** If the user writes in varied lengths, maintain the variation.
5. **Removing personality quirks.** Those quirks ARE the voice. Keep them.

---

## Default Voice (When No Samples Available)

If the user provides no samples and no guidance:

- Sentence length: Mixed (short for emphasis, medium for explanation)
- Vocabulary: Professional but accessible
- Formality: Conversational
- Humor: Light when appropriate, never forced
- Perspective: First person
- Emotional openness: Moderate
- Paragraph structure: Tight (1-2 sentences)
- Signature patterns: None (neutral)

This produces a "professional human" voice that works for most contexts but should be customized as soon as samples are available.
